\abstract{Spatial domain identification methods are usually compared by small differences in adjusted Rand index (ARI),
mostly on one tissue and from a single run. We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines
on \nSections{} annotated sections from 10x Visium, STARmap and MERFISH, separating each learned embedding from its clustering
step. With the same clustering for every embedding, a training-free neighbourhood-smoothing baseline had a higher mean ARI than
\nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators and could not be separated from the other \nRobUnres{}, including BASS and BANKSY.
The clustering step was a large source of variation: on fixed embeddings, refitting the same Gaussian mixture from different
random starts changed ARI by \lotMean{} on average (up to \lotMax), and the default fit of R mclust depended on its random seed
(up to \mcsSeedMax). Keeping the most likely of 20 fits helped on cortex but not on single-cell MERFISH data, where likelihood
and ARI were unrelated. HiSTaR did not reproduce its published accuracy with its official code, and released code of one method
selects its reported epoch using the labels. We recommend reporting the clustering protocol and its variance, never selecting
runs with labels, and including training-free baselines.}

\keywords{spatial transcriptomics, spatial domains, clustering, reproducibility, benchmarking}

\section{Introduction}

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at thousands of locations in a tissue section
while retaining their coordinates. Identifying \emph{spatial domains}, regions with coherent
expression such as cortical layers, is one of the first analyses of such data, and many methods
have been proposed for it, from Bayesian models \citep{zhao2021bayesspace} and
neighbourhood-augmented features \citep{singhal2024banksy} to graph neural networks
\citep{hu2021spagcn,dong2022stagate,long2023graphst} and hierarchical graph variational
autoencoders such as HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar}.

Most of these methods are evaluated on the twelve manually annotated human dorsolateral prefrontal
cortex (DLPFC) sections \citep{maynard2021dlpfc} and report median adjusted Rand index (ARI) \citep{hubert1985ari}
improvements of a few hundredths over previous methods \citep{dong2022stagate,long2023graphst,yu2025histar}. Several
(for example GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR) share the same last step: the learned embedding is clustered once with the
EEE Gaussian mixture of the R package mclust \citep{scrucca2016mclust}. Benchmarks have found that rankings depend strongly
on the dataset \citep{yuan2024benchmark,nar2025bench}, that deep methods vary with the random seed \citep{hu2024gbbench}, that
replacing each method's clustering algorithm changes results \citep{nar2025bench}, and that preprocessing and clustering
choices often matter more than architectural novelty \citep{explanatory2026}. Simple training-free pipelines can also be
competitive \citep{nichepca2025,do2026graphbg}. What these studies do not measure directly is how much of the variation
comes from the clustering step itself when the embedding is held fixed.

We benchmark \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines along three axes. \emph{Accuracy}: how do the
methods compare across three technologies when their embeddings are clustered in the same way, and how does this change with
each method's own clustering? \emph{Robustness}: on a \emph{fixed} embedding, how much does ARI change with the random
initialisation of the mixture model and with the random seed of R mclust, and does selecting fits by likelihood help?
\emph{Reproducibility}: do published results reproduce with the official code (here HiSTaR's DLPFC results), and does
released code select models using the labels? The study design is summarised in \figref{fig:overview}. We find that a
standard neighbourhood-smoothing baseline is among the most accurate methods, that the clustering step varies more than
most differences between methods, and that released code can use the labels for model selection.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data}
\textbf{DLPFC (10x Visium).} Twelve sections from three donors \citep{maynard2021dlpfc}; seven
annotated layers (five for donor 2), \texttt{layer\_guess\_reordered}.
\textbf{STARmap mouse medial prefrontal cortex.} Three sections (about 1050 cells, 166 genes) with
four annotated layers (L1, L2/3, L5, L6) \citep{wang2018starmap}.
\textbf{MERFISH mouse hypothalamus.} Animal 1, five sections from Bregma $-0.04$ to $-0.24$ (about
5500 cells, 155 genes) with eight annotated regions \citep{moffitt2018merfish}. The STARmap and
MERFISH annotations are those distributed with the BASS analyses \citep{li2022bass}, one of the benchmarked methods
(see Limitations). In total, \nSections{} sections from three technologies (Supplementary Table~\STqc; Supplementary Note~\SNdata).

\subsection{Preprocessing}
DLPFC: genes in fewer than 50 spots or with fewer than 10 counts were removed, 2000 highly
variable genes were selected (Seurat v3 flavour), counts were library-size normalised,
log-transformed and scaled, and reduced to 200 principal components (PCs). STARmap and MERFISH
(targeted panels): all genes, same normalisation, 50 PCs. All methods received the same input
where they accept one; BANKSY used the log-normalised genes, and SpaGCN, GraphST and the other published methods
used their own documented preprocessing of the same raw counts. The true number of domains was given to every method,
and unannotated spots were excluded from evaluation.

\subsection{Methods}
Methods were chosen to cover the main families of spatial domain identification with maintained public code that runs on
CPU: deep graph encoders, statistical and neighbourhood methods, and Bayesian models. Every method was run with its official
code and default or tutorial settings on one CPU thread; none was tuned per dataset. Versions, settings and deviations from
the tutorials (for example the neighbourhood radius, expressed in spot spacings so that it transfers across technologies) are
listed in Supplementary Table~\STmethods.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep graph encoders.} HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar} (PyTorch port of the official code with the published
defaults: cross-level weight 0.3, 200+200 epochs), GraphST \citep{long2023graphst} (official package and tutorial: 600 epochs,
20-PC projection, R mclust; with and without its label refinement), SpaGCN \citep{hu2021spagcn} (tutorial without histology,
Louvain resolution search to the true number of domains), STAGATE \citep{dong2022stagate}, SEDR \citep{xu2024sedr},
SpaceFlow \citep{ren2022spaceflow}, CCST \citep{li2022ccst}, Spatial-MGCN \citep{wang2023spatialmgcn} and DeepST
\citep{xu2022deepst}. Spatial-MGCN's released test script reports the epoch with the highest ARI against the annotation;
in the benchmark we report its final epoch (label-free) and give the label-selected value separately.
\item \textbf{Statistical and neighbourhood methods.} BANKSY \citep{singhal2024banksy} (official \texttt{pybanksy},
18 neighbours, $m_{\max}=1$, 20 PCs; $\lambda=0.8$, recommended for domain segmentation, and $\lambda=0.2$, recommended for cell
typing, included for reference), NichePCA \citep{nichepca2025} (a published training-free method: neighbour aggregation and PCA),
and the Bayesian models BASS \citep{li2022bass} and BayesSpace \citep{zhao2021bayesspace}, which cluster internally. BayesSpace
requires a spot lattice and was run on the Visium sections only.
\item \textbf{Training-free baselines.} \emph{Linear diffusion} is standard neighbourhood smoothing (graph Laplacian
smoothing): the top 30 PCs are smoothed on the spatial six-nearest-neighbour graph,
$H^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha)H^{(t)}+\alpha D^{-1}AH^{(t)}$, $\alpha=0.5$, $T=10$. \emph{AnisoST} uses the same scheme with edge weights
recomputed at every step, $w_{ij}^{(t)}=\exp(-\|h_i^{(t)}-h_j^{(t)}\|^2/\kappa^{(t)})$ (Perona--Malik-type edge-preserving
diffusion \citep{perona1990}; $\kappa^{(t)}$ the median squared edge difference); a similar idea has been used inside a deep
model \citep{wang2024pearlst}. \emph{PCA} without smoothing is the non-spatial reference. All baseline settings were fixed on
DLPFC donor 1 before any other data were analysed (sensitivity: Supplementary Fig.~\SFsens).
\end{itemize}
Seeds: three per section for most methods; one for STAGATE, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and BASS in the published pipelines, and one
run per section for BayesSpace (Supplementary Table~\STcov).

\paragraph{Neutrality.} The authors developed AnisoST, and linear diffusion and PCA are its ablations; the authors did not
develop any of the published methods. To limit bias, all baseline settings were frozen on four development sections, every
published method was run with its authors' defaults, and all comparisons are repeated without the development sections.

\subsection{Clustering protocols}
\emph{Default clustering}: the clustering step of each method's official pipeline. This is R mclust (EEE) for GraphST, STAGATE and
SEDR; a 3-start scikit-learn Gaussian mixture with tied covariance \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn} for HiSTaR, BANKSY and the three
baselines; Leiden or $k$-means for SpaceFlow, NichePCA, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST; and the internal model for SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace. \emph{Matched clustering} (primary analysis): every embedding clustered with the same tied-covariance Gaussian mixture
(the mclust EEE model) from 20 random starts, keeping the fit with the highest log-likelihood (label-free). SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace output no embedding and keep their own clustering under both protocols.
\emph{Initialisation experiment}: one seed-0 embedding per section and method, refitted 20 times with a single random start
each (\lotNMethods{} methods and baselines that output an embedding). \emph{R mclust experiment}: R mclust 6.0.1
(\texttt{Mclust(Z, G = k, modelNames = "EEE")}) on the same embeddings, (i) with its default initialisation under ten R random
seeds and fixed row order, (ii) initialised on all observations (\texttt{initialization = list(subset = 1:n)}), and (iii) the
same after adding Gaussian noise with 1\% of each feature's standard deviation, five times.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}
ARI against the manual annotation is the primary metric; NMI gave the same ranking of methods (Supplementary Table~\STnmi), and
further agreement, spatial and label-free metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics{} (definitions in Supplementary
Note~\SNmetrics). For every method and section we average over seeds. Sections are not independent (the 12 DLPFC sections come
from three donors; the STARmap and MERFISH sections come from one experiment each), so the unit of analysis is the group:
\statClusters{} groups (three DLPFC donors, STARmap, MERFISH). For each method we estimate the difference in mean ARI from
linear diffusion, the reference, which was chosen after the means were seen, so these comparisons are exploratory. Intervals
come from a hierarchical bootstrap (\statBoot{} resamples of groups, then of sections within a group; the estimate is the
equal-weight mean of group means) with 95\% percentile intervals (Supplementary Note~\SNstats). A method is called equivalent
to the reference if the 90\% interval lies within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI, about the seed-to-seed standard deviation. Ranks and the
probability of ranking first come from the same bootstrap. The analysis is repeated without the development sections (DLPFC donor 1
and the first section of STARmap and MERFISH). With five groups the intervals are wide and no correction for multiple comparisons
is applied; we therefore interpret only consistent patterns. Run time and peak memory were measured in a standardised runner that
gives every method the same input (process-tree sampling, one CPU thread per job; Supplementary Note~\SNcompute); its accuracy
agrees with the published pipelines to within the differences listed in Supplementary Table~\STagree.

\section{Results}

\subsection{A training-free smoothing baseline is among the most accurate methods}
With matched clustering, linear diffusion had a mean ARI of \meanLinDlpfc, \meanLinStarmap{} and \meanLinMerfish{} on DLPFC,
STARmap and MERFISH (\figref{fig:accuracy}A; per-dataset values for both protocols in Supplementary Table~\STari). Across all
methods and both protocols, the highest dataset means were reached by \bestDlpfc{} on DLPFC (\bestDlpfcVal), \bestStarmap{} on
STARmap (\bestStarmapVal) and \bestMerfish{} on MERFISH (\bestMerfishVal). Linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than
\nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators (95\% interval above zero; \figref{fig:accuracy}B; Supplementary Table~\STstatsrob), and
this held for \nRobBetterTest{} of them without the development sections. Five comparisons were unresolved: BASS ($\Delta$
\robDBass, 95\% interval \robLoBass{} to \robHiBass), BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$ (\robDBanksyHi, \robLoBanksyHi{} to \robHiBanksyHi),
HiSTaR (\robDHistar, \robLoHistar{} to \robHiHistar), SEDR (\robDSedr, \robLoSedr{} to \robHiSedr) and AnisoST (\robDAniso,
\robLoAniso{} to \robHiAniso). No comparator was equivalent to the baseline within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI, and BASS was ahead on
STARmap and MERFISH. Averaging the three dataset means with equal weight instead of the five groups puts BASS (\overallBass) slightly
above linear diffusion (\overallLin). In the bootstrap, linear diffusion ranked first in \pBestLin{} of the resamples (95\% rank
interval \rankLoLin--\rankHiLin{} among \rankMethods{} methods) and BASS in \pBestBass{} (\figref{fig:accuracy}C; Supplementary
Table~\STranks). With each method's default clustering the picture was similar but more favourable to the baseline: \nStdBetter{}
of \nStdTot{} comparisons favoured it and \nStdUnres{} (BASS and BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$) were unresolved (Supplementary
Table~\STstats). Example partitions are shown in \figref{fig:maps}.

Seed-to-seed variability differed between methods: HiSTaR's standard deviation of ARI was 0.047 on DLPFC and 0.18 on STARmap,
against 0.003--0.04 for the training-free baselines (Supplementary Table~\STseeds). The median time per section on one CPU
thread ranged from under 30 s for the baselines and BANKSY to about 40 min for CCST (Supplementary Table~\STres).
The edge-preserving variant AnisoST did not improve on linear diffusion overall (MERFISH: 0.363 vs 0.497), although on DLPFC it
was more accurate on spots at layer boundaries (by 0.025, 9 of 12 sections, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.034$, uncorrected;
Supplementary Fig.~\SFboundary); at single-cell resolution neighbouring cells often differ by cell type, so an edge-preserving
rule blocks the smoothing needed to reveal regions.

\subsection{Random initialisation of the clustering step changes ARI on a fixed embedding}
\figref{fig:robust}A shows the ARI of 20 single-start fits of the same mixture model on each fixed embedding. Across the
\lotN{} embeddings of \lotNMethods{} methods and baselines, the ARI range within an embedding was \lotMean{} on average and reached
\lotMax{} (\lotWorstMethod, \lotWorstSection). The training-free baselines were not immune: the mean range was \lotMeanLin{} for
linear diffusion, against \lotLo{} for \lotLoName{} and \lotHi{} for \lotHiName{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery). The best of
the 20 fits by ARI exceeded the average single fit by \lotOracle: choosing a clustering run by its agreement with the labels,
explicitly or implicitly by trying several seeds, would inflate a result by more than the few hundredths typically reported
between successive methods. Every fit for three representative methods is shown in Supplementary Fig.~\SFlottery.

\subsection{The default fit of R mclust depends on a hidden random seed}
For data with more than 2000 observations, R mclust initialises the EM algorithm by hierarchical agglomeration on a random subset
of 2000 observations drawn with R's random number generator, so its default fit depends on the R seed. The published pipelines fix
this seed (2020 in STAGATE and GraphST, 2023 in SEDR), which makes their result reproducible but tied to an arbitrary choice. Across
the \mcsNBig{} embeddings with more than 2000 spots or cells, changing only the R seed changed ARI by \mcsSeedMeanBig{} on average
(up to \mcsSeedMax; \figref{fig:robust}B), and the range exceeded 0.1 for \mcsSeedGt\% of them; the STARmap sections, with about
1050 cells, were initialised on all cells and did not vary. Initialising on all observations removed the dependence on the seed and
on the row order; the remaining sensitivity to 1\% noise was \mcsJitMean{} on average (up to \mcsJitMax). The full-data fit fell
outside the range of the ten seeded fits for \mcsFullOutside\% of the embeddings (Supplementary Table~\STmclust). An earlier
version of this experiment attributed the variation to the order of the spots; permuting the rows changes which observations are
sampled, so it is the same effect.

\subsection{Selecting fits by likelihood helps on cortex but not on single-cell data}
A natural fix is to run many starts and keep the most likely fit. On the same embedding and model, the best-likelihood fit beat the
average single start for \lotBLUp{} of \lotNMethods{} methods and was worse for \lotBLDown{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery).
Replacing each method's default clustering by matched clustering changed mean ARI by more than 0.005 upwards for \nGainPos{} of
\nGainTot{} methods and downwards for \nGainNeg{} (\figref{fig:matched}; Supplementary Table~\STgain); for example HiSTaR gained
\gainHistar{} (\gainPHistar) and NichePCA \gainNichepca{} (\gainPNichepca), whereas SEDR changed by \gainSedr{} (\gainPSedr) and CCST
by \gainCcst{} (\gainPCcst), all uncorrected. The reason is visible in \figref{fig:robust}C: likelihood and ARI were positively
related on DLPFC (median Spearman $\rho$ \rhoDlpfcMed) but not on MERFISH (median $\rho$ \rhoMerfishMed; negative for
\rhoNegMerfish{} of \lotNMethods{} methods), where the best-fitting mixture can separate cell types rather than anatomical regions.
Better optimisation of the mixture model is therefore not the same as better domains, and the clustering protocol alone can
reorder methods.

\subsection{HiSTaR's published DLPFC accuracy was not reproduced}
With the official code and defaults, HiSTaR reached a median ARI over the twelve DLPFC sections of
0.509 (mean 0.491, 95\% bootstrap CI over sections 0.454--0.525) with the 3-start GMM, and 0.449 (mean, seed 0)
when its embedding was clustered with R mclust as in the official pipeline, compared with a reported
median of 0.65 over the twelve sections and 0.718 on section 151673 \citep{yu2025histar}. Fixing a bug that applies random masking at inference did not change accuracy (0.487; Supplementary Table~\SThistar).
Possible causes include per-section tuning of the loss weights (described in the HiSTaR supplement), unpublished preprocessing
and the choice of seed; the results above show that the clustering step alone can produce per-section differences of this size.

\subsection{Released code of one method selects the reported epoch using the labels}

Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts (\texttt{DLPFC\_test.py}, \texttt{HBC\_test.py}) compute ARI against the annotation at every
training epoch and keep the best epoch for reporting. Applying the same selection rule in our runs (seed 0, \mgcnLeakN{} sections),
this label-selected epoch had a mean ARI of \mgcnOracleDlpfc{} on DLPFC, \mgcnOracleStarmap{} on STARmap and \mgcnOracleMerfish{}
on MERFISH, against \mgcnFinalDlpfc, \mgcnFinalStarmap{} and \mgcnFinalMerfish{} for the final epoch used in our benchmark, an
inflation of \mgcnLeak{} on average (\mgcnLeakStarmap{} on STARmap; Supplementary Table~\STepoch). Selecting a run, epoch or seed by
agreement with the labels is a form of label leakage that makes a method look competitive with the training-free baselines
(\mgcnOracleDlpfc{} against \meanLinDlpfc{} on DLPFC) when its label-free result is not. An automated screen of the released
repositories of 14 methods for other patterns of label use, with every candidate line read by hand, is reported in Supplementary
Table~\STaudit{} and Supplementary Note~\SNaudit.

\section{Discussion}
We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines on \nSections{} sections from three
technologies and separated the learned embedding from the clustering step. When all embeddings were clustered in the same way,
standard neighbourhood smoothing had a higher mean ARI than most methods and could not be separated from the best of the rest
(BASS, BANKSY, HiSTaR, SEDR and AnisoST); no method was shown to be equivalent to it. The clustering step was a larger source of
variation than most differences between methods: random initialisation of the same mixture model spanned \lotMean{} ARI on a fixed
embedding, the default fit of R mclust, used by several widely used methods, depended on a fixed but arbitrary random seed, and
better likelihood did not reliably mean better domains. Reproducibility was limited both by the clustering step and by choices in
released code, including selecting a reported epoch with the labels.

Why does simple smoothing do so well? On layered tissue such as cortex, domains are large, contiguous regions, and averaging each
spot with its neighbours removes most of the sampling noise that separates spots of the same layer; much of what a graph encoder
learns may be this smoothing. On single-cell MERFISH data, neighbouring cells often belong to different cell types, and the
best-fitting mixture can then describe cell types rather than regions, which explains why likelihood and ARI disagree there. For a
practitioner, the results suggest starting with a smoothing baseline, choosing the clustering step deliberately and reporting its
variance. These results agree with recent benchmarks \citep{explanatory2026,hu2024gbbench,nar2025bench} and add a direct measurement
of clustering variance on fixed embeddings for \lotNMethods{} methods across three technologies.

\paragraph{Recommendations.}
(1) Report the clustering protocol: algorithm, implementation and version, initialisation, number of starts, random seeds and how
they were chosen (\figref{fig:robust}A,B).
(2) Never select seeds, epochs or runs by agreement with the labels (Supplementary Table~\STepoch).
(3) Report distributions over training seeds and over clustering runs or R seeds, with per-section values (Supplementary
Tables~\STseeds--\STmclust).
(4) Compare encoders under the same clustering, and include training-free baselines such as neighbourhood smoothing
(\figref{fig:matched}).
(5) Report run time, memory and seed variance alongside accuracy (Supplementary Table~\STres).
(6) Release per-run results and seeds so that variance can be recomputed.
(7) If fits are selected, use a label-free criterion and report how often it agrees with the annotation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All methods were run with default or published settings, without a tuning budget, so the results do not show what each method can
reach with its authors' own tuning (we could not reproduce HiSTaR's per-section tuning). The reference baseline was chosen after
the means were seen, and the comparisons with it are exploratory. Four methods were run with one seed in the published pipelines
and BayesSpace on Visium only (Supplementary Table~\STcov), so seed variance is unavailable for them. The initialisation and R
mclust experiments use one seed-0 embedding per section and method and do not include training-seed variance. The three datasets
are brain tissues with laminar or regional structure; Slide-seq, Stereo-seq, Xenium and Visium HD data, larger sections and
non-laminar tissues are not covered, and no simulated data were used. The STARmap and MERFISH annotations were distributed with the
BASS analyses, and BASS was among the most accurate methods on these data; annotations may also have been derived with methods
similar to the ones evaluated. The number of domains was given to every method. All runs used one CPU thread, so run times are not
representative of GPU use. ARI is the primary metric; other metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics.
