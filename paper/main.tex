\abstract{Identifying spatial domains, tissue regions of coherent gene expression such as cortical layers, is a first step in
analysing spatial transcriptomics data. Many methods,
from Bayesian models to graph neural networks, report gains of a few hundredths in adjusted Rand index (ARI), mostly on one human
cortex dataset and from single runs whose clustering step is rarely examined. How much of such a gain reflects the method,
rather than the clustering step, the tissue or the chosen run, is unknown. We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three
training-free baselines on \nSections{} sections from four technologies, clustering every learned embedding in the same way and
refitting the clustering step on fixed embeddings. A smoothing baseline was more accurate than \nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{}
comparators, was not separated from the rest, and led only on brain tissue. On a
fixed embedding, random restarts of one mixture model changed ARI by \lotMean{} on average (up to \lotMax). A pre-registered simulation
showed that smoothing helps large domains and hurts small ones, and whole-brain data replicated the clustering variance. Released code
of one method selected its reported epoch using the labels. We recommend reporting the clustering protocol and its variance,
label-free model selection and multi-tissue baselines.}

\keywords{spatial transcriptomics, spatial domains, clustering, reproducibility, benchmarking}

\section{Introduction}

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at thousands of locations in a tissue section while retaining their coordinates,
from spots about 55~\textmu m wide on 10x Visium arrays \citep{maynard2021dlpfc} to single cells in imaging assays such as STARmap and
MERFISH \citep{wang2018starmap,moffitt2018merfish} and binned DNA-nanoball arrays in Stereo-seq \citep{chen2022mosta}. Identifying
\emph{spatial domains}, regions with coherent expression such as cortical layers or tumour regions, is one of the first analyses of such
data, and later steps, such as finding region-specific genes or comparing tissues, build on it. Because the true domains are rarely
known, methods are judged by their agreement with expert annotations, almost always measured by the adjusted Rand index (ARI)
\citep{hubert1985ari}.

Many methods have been proposed. Bayesian and probabilistic models cluster spots directly under a spatial prior: BayesSpace places a
Potts-type prior on neighbouring spots of the Visium lattice \citep{zhao2021bayesspace}, BASS infers cell types and domains jointly in a
hierarchical model that can span several sections \citep{li2022bass}, and PRECAST combines a probabilistic embedding with clustering and
alignment of several sections \citep{liu2023precast}. SpatialPCA adds a spatial kernel to dimension reduction \citep{shang2022spatialpca}.
Neighbourhood-feature methods augment each spot or cell with information from its surroundings: BANKSY appends the neighbourhood mean and
gradient of expression \citep{singhal2024banksy}, CellCharter aggregates features over neighbourhoods of increasing size
\citep{varrone2024cellcharter}, and MENDER summarises the cell-type composition around each cell at several ranges \citep{yuan2024mender}.
The largest group are graph neural networks that learn an embedding of each spot from its expression and that of its neighbours: SpaGCN
with graph convolution \citep{hu2021spagcn}, STAGATE with a graph attention autoencoder \citep{dong2022stagate}, GraphST with graph
contrastive learning \citep{long2023graphst}, SEDR with a masked graph autoencoder \citep{xu2024sedr}, HiSTaR with a hierarchical graph
variational autoencoder \citep{yu2025histar}, and others \citep{ren2022spaceflow,li2022ccst,wang2023spatialmgcn,xu2022deepst}. Training-free
pipelines have also been proposed: NichePCA aggregates neighbour expression before principal component analysis \citep{nichepca2025}, and
GraphBG uses spectral graph convolutions inside a Bayesian model \citep{do2026graphbg}.

Most of these methods are evaluated on the twelve manually annotated sections of human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)
\citep{maynard2021dlpfc}, and successive papers report median ARI improvements of a few hundredths
\citep{dong2022stagate,long2023graphst,yu2025histar}. Three features of this practice make such differences hard to interpret. First, a
learned method is a pipeline of two steps, an embedding and a clustering of that embedding, and several methods (for example GraphST,
STAGATE and SEDR) share the same last step: the embedding is clustered once with the EEE Gaussian mixture of the R package mclust
\citep{scrucca2016mclust} under a fixed random seed. A reported number therefore mixes the quality of the embedding with one draw of the
clustering step. Second, DLPFC is a layered tissue with large, contiguous domains, so a method tuned and tested on it may not transfer to
tissues with smaller or interleaved domains. Third, results usually come from a single run, so the choice of run, epoch or seed is part
of the result.

Independent benchmarks have addressed parts of this. They found that rankings depend strongly on the dataset
\citep{yuan2024benchmark,nar2025bench}, that deep methods vary with the random seed \citep{hu2024gbbench}, that replacing each method's
clustering algorithm changes results \citep{nar2025bench}, and that preprocessing and clustering choices often matter more than
architectural novelty \citep{explanatory2026}; simple training-free pipelines can also be competitive \citep{nichepca2025,do2026graphbg}.
Three questions remain open. (i) These studies compare methods with their own or with substituted clustering algorithms, but they do not
measure directly how much ARI varies when the embedding is held fixed and only the clustering step is repeated. (ii) Why simple smoothing
is competitive, and on which tissues, has to our knowledge not been tested in an experiment that varies only the geometry of the domains.
(iii) Whether released code uses the annotation to select the reported model has, to our knowledge, not been checked systematically.

Here we address these questions with a benchmark designed to separate the two steps of each method (\figref{fig:overview}). We run
\nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines on \nSections{} sections from four technologies and three tissue types.
For question (i), we cluster every embedding with the same \emph{matched} clustering, a 20-start Gaussian mixture that keeps the most
likely fit without looking at the labels, alongside each method's own clustering; and we hold one embedding per section and method fixed
and refit it 20 times from single random starts and with R mclust under ten random seeds. For question (ii), we compare a standard
neighbourhood-smoothing baseline, linear diffusion, with every method on each tissue, and test the resulting explanation in a
pre-registered simulation that varies only the size and arrangement of domains and on whole-brain MERFISH sections whose domains come from
registration to an anatomical atlas rather than from clustering. For question (iii), we try to reproduce one recent method's published
accuracy and screen the released code of the benchmarked methods for label use.

Several design choices keep these comparisons fair. The unit of statistical analysis is the donor or dataset, not the section, with
intervals from a hierarchical bootstrap; the baseline settings were frozen on four development sections before any other data were
analysed; every published method ran with its authors' defaults, and none was developed by us; the simulation's hypotheses and decision
rules were committed before any simulated tissue existed; and every number in the paper is regenerated by scripts from per-run records.
Relative to the benchmarks above, the new elements are the direct measurement of clustering-step variance on fixed embeddings, the
controlled test of domain size, and the audit of released code for label-based model selection. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{C1, accuracy depends on the tissue.} With matched clustering, linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than \nRobBetter{} of
\nRobTot{} comparators and could not be separated from the other \nRobUnres; BASS and the baseline were the most likely to rank first
(\pBestBass{} and \pBestLin{} of bootstrap resamples). The lead came from brain tissue, where the baseline exceeded the average comparator
by \dBrain{} ARI; on breast cancer and mouse embryo it trailed by \dOtherAbs.
\item \textbf{C2, the clustering step varies more than the differences usually reported between methods.} On fixed embeddings, 20
single-start fits of the same mixture changed ARI by \lotMean{} on average (up to \lotMax), and changing only the random seed of R mclust
changed it by \mcsSeedMeanBig{} on average (up to \mcsSeedMax); both effects replicated on whole-brain data.
\item \textbf{C3, a better-fitting clustering is not a better domain map.} Log-likelihood tracked ARI on cortex (median Spearman $\rho$
\rhoDlpfcMed{} on DLPFC) but not on the other tissues (\rhoMerfishMed{} on MERFISH), and replacing each method's clustering by matched
clustering raised mean ARI by more than 0.005 for \nGainPos{} of \nGainTot{} methods and lowered it for \nGainNeg.
\item \textbf{C4, smoothing helps large domains and hurts small ones.} In the simulation, the advantage of linear diffusion over PCA fell
from \simDAone{} ARI for wide layers to \simDAseven{} for narrow ones and \simDBthree{} for small random patches; on whole-brain sections,
whose anatomical divisions are large, linear diffusion reached \alLin{} against \alPca{} for PCA.
\item \textbf{C5, published results depend on choices in released code.} Our port of HiSTaR reached a median DLPFC ARI of 0.509 against a
reported 0.65, and Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts select the reported epoch by ARI against the labels, which raised its DLPFC ARI by
\mgcnLeakDlpfc.
\end{enumerate}
From these results we derive recommendations for evaluating and reporting spatial domain methods (Discussion).

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data}
\textbf{DLPFC (10x Visium).} Twelve sections from three donors \citep{maynard2021dlpfc}; seven
annotated layers (five for donor 2), \texttt{layer\_guess\_reordered}.
\textbf{STARmap mouse medial prefrontal cortex.} Three sections (about 1050 cells, 166 genes) with
four annotated layers (L1, L2/3, L5, L6) \citep{wang2018starmap}.
\textbf{MERFISH mouse hypothalamus.} Animal 1, five sections from Bregma $-0.04$ to $-0.24$ (about
5500 cells, 155 genes) with eight annotated regions \citep{moffitt2018merfish}. The STARmap and
MERFISH annotations are those distributed with the BASS analyses \citep{li2022bass}, one of the benchmarked methods
(see Limitations).
\textbf{Human breast cancer (10x Visium).} Block A Section 1 of the 10x Genomics demonstration data \citep{tenx2020brca}
(3798 spots), with the 20 regions (ductal and lobular carcinoma \emph{in situ}, invasive ductal carcinoma, tumour edge and healthy
tissue) of the annotation distributed with the SEDR analyses \citep{xu2024sedr}.
\textbf{Mouse embryo (Stereo-seq).} Three sections of the MOSTA atlas \citep{chen2022mosta} at bin50 resolution (about 25~\textmu m):
E9.5 E1S1 (5913 bins, 12 annotated tissues), E10.5 E2S1 (8494 bins, 18) and E10.5 E1S1 (18,408 bins, 13), with the published
tissue annotation. These two datasets were added after the analysis of the first three was complete, without changing any shared setting.
In total, \nSections{} sections from four technologies (Supplementary Table~\STqc; Supplementary Note~\SNdata).
Two further datasets were added after this analysis was complete and are analysed separately (see below).
\textbf{Mouse whole brain (MERFISH).} Three coronal sections of one adult mouse from the Allen Brain Cell Atlas (Zhuang-ABCA-1
\citep{zhang2023merfishbrain,yao2023abca}): of the \alNCand{} sections with more than 20,000 cells, those at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile
of the anterior--posterior position (\alCellsLo--\alCellsHi{} cells, 1122 genes). Domains are the divisions of the Allen Common Coordinate
Framework (CCF) assigned to each cell by registration of the section to the atlas, not by clustering of the expression data; divisions with at
least 1\% of a section's cells were kept (\alKLo--\alKHi{} domains), and the other cells were not scored.
\textbf{Simulated tissue.} Square lattices of 120 $\times$ 120 spots with six domains whose expression profiles are those of layers L1--L6 of
DLPFC section 151507 (negative-binomial counts, 5000 genes). In geometry A the six domains repeat $m\in\{1,2,4,7\}$ times as parallel bands
(about 20, 10, 5 and 3 spots wide); in geometry B each $w\times w$ block, $w\in\{3,5,8,12,20\}$, takes one of the six domains at random
(\figref{fig:newdata}A). Five tissues per setting (Supplementary Note~\SNnewdata).

\subsection{Preprocessing}
DLPFC, breast cancer and Stereo-seq (whole transcriptome; Stereo-seq bins without counts removed): genes in fewer than 50 spots or with fewer than 10 counts were removed, 2000 highly
variable genes were selected (Seurat v3 flavour), counts were library-size normalised,
log-transformed and scaled, and reduced to 200 principal components (PCs). STARmap and MERFISH
(targeted panels): all genes, same normalisation, 50 PCs. All methods received the same input
where they accept one; BANKSY used the log-normalised genes, and SpaGCN, GraphST and the other published methods
used their own documented preprocessing of the same raw counts. The true number of domains was given to every method,
and unannotated spots were excluded from evaluation.

\subsection{Methods}
Methods were chosen to cover the main families of spatial domain identification with maintained public code that runs on
CPU: deep graph encoders, statistical and neighbourhood methods, and Bayesian models. Every method was run with its released
code (HiSTaR was run as our PyTorch port and CCST as our re-implementation, because the released code does not run in the shared environment) and default or tutorial settings on one CPU thread (except the reruns on the second server, see below); none was tuned by us per dataset, but some tutorial settings are technology-specific (GraphST's Stereo-seq mode; BASS selects its own 3000 spatially variable genes and reuses its DLPFC settings on breast cancer and Stereo-seq; Supplementary Table~\STmethods). Versions, settings and deviations from
the tutorials (for example the neighbourhood radius, expressed in spot spacings so that it transfers across technologies) are
listed in Supplementary Table~\STmethods.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep graph encoders.} HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar} (PyTorch port of the official code with the published
defaults: cross-level weight 0.3, 200+200 epochs), GraphST \citep{long2023graphst} (official package and tutorial: 600 epochs,
20-PC projection, R mclust; with and without its label refinement; on Stereo-seq its documented setting for Stereo-seq data,
\texttt{datatype='Stereo'}), SpaGCN \citep{hu2021spagcn} (tutorial without histology,
Louvain resolution search to the true number of domains), STAGATE \citep{dong2022stagate}, SEDR \citep{xu2024sedr},
SpaceFlow \citep{ren2022spaceflow}, CCST \citep{li2022ccst}, Spatial-MGCN \citep{wang2023spatialmgcn} and DeepST
\citep{xu2022deepst}. Spatial-MGCN's released test script reports the epoch with the highest ARI against the annotation;
in the benchmark we report its final epoch (label-free) and give the label-selected value separately.
\item \textbf{Statistical and neighbourhood methods.} BANKSY \citep{singhal2024banksy} (official \texttt{pybanksy},
18 neighbours, $m_{\max}=1$, 20 PCs; $\lambda=0.8$, recommended for domain segmentation, and $\lambda=0.2$, recommended for cell
typing, included for reference), NichePCA \citep{nichepca2025} (a published training-free method: neighbour aggregation and PCA),
and the Bayesian models BASS \citep{li2022bass} and BayesSpace \citep{zhao2021bayesspace}, which cluster internally. BayesSpace
was run on the Visium sections only: its square-lattice mode for other platforms was designed for larger spots and was not used on the Stereo-seq bin50 grid, and STARmap and MERFISH have no lattice.
\item \textbf{Training-free baselines.} \emph{Linear diffusion} is standard neighbourhood smoothing (graph Laplacian
smoothing): the top 30 PCs are smoothed on the spatial six-nearest-neighbour graph,
$H^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha)H^{(t)}+\alpha D^{-1}AH^{(t)}$, $\alpha=0.5$, $T=10$. \emph{AnisoST} uses the same scheme with edge weights
recomputed at every step, $w_{ij}^{(t)}=\exp(-\|h_i^{(t)}-h_j^{(t)}\|^2/\kappa^{(t)})$ (Perona--Malik-type edge-preserving
diffusion \citep{perona1990}; $\kappa^{(t)}$ the median squared edge difference); a similar idea has been used inside a deep
model \citep{wang2024pearlst}. \emph{PCA} without smoothing is the non-spatial reference. All baseline settings were fixed on
DLPFC donor 1 before any other data were analysed (sensitivity: Supplementary Figure~\SFsens).
\end{itemize}
Seeds: three per section for most methods; one for STAGATE, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and BASS in the published pipelines and for
GraphST on DLPFC, and one run per section for BayesSpace (Supplementary Table~\STcov). The breast cancer and Stereo-seq sections
were run only in the standardised runner described below, with three seeds per method (two for CCST on the largest Stereo-seq
section, where one run takes more than 5 h; one run for BayesSpace).

\emph{Memory-capped jobs.} On these two datasets jobs ran one at a time with a memory
cap of \memCap{} GB on a 15 GB machine; the earlier runs on the first three datasets had no cap (the largest, BayesSpace on DLPFC,
used 12.5 GB). BASS, GraphST, NichePCA, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST exceeded the cap on the largest Stereo-seq section (18,408 bins), and
DeepST seeds 1 and 2 on E10.5 E2S1; these 17 jobs were rerun with the same code and settings on a second server with 125 GB RAM
(CPU only, a different processor; the thread count was not restricted). Failed jobs were repeated with unchanged settings; the only change was the GraphST wrapper below. Their accuracy and peak memory are reported with the rest; their run times are not compared
with the others. For GraphST, the call to R mclust was wrapped for the newer software stack of that server (the wrapper is now part of the adapter for every host; the earlier GraphST records used the original call); it gave identical labels
on two test embeddings (Supplementary Note~\SNcompute). The reruns gave 20 records for the 17 jobs, because three GraphST jobs failed once before the wrapper was added and were repeated. DeepST on E10.5 E2S1 has seed 0 from the benchmark machine and seeds 1 and 2 from the second server (ARI \deepstEtwoSeedZero{}, \deepstEtwoSeedOne{}, \deepstEtwoSeedTwo{}), so host and seed are confounded for that section.

\paragraph{Neutrality.} The authors developed AnisoST, and linear diffusion and PCA are its ablations; the authors did not
develop any of the published methods. To limit bias, all baseline settings were frozen on the four sections of DLPFC donor 1, every
published method was run with its authors' defaults, and all comparisons are repeated without the six development sections
(those four and the first STARmap and MERFISH sections, used to test the pipelines).

\subsection{Clustering protocols}
\emph{Default clustering}: the clustering step of each method's pipeline. This is R mclust (EEE) for GraphST, STAGATE and
SEDR; a 3-start scikit-learn Gaussian mixture with tied covariance \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn} for the three baselines and, as our choice, for
HiSTaR and BANKSY (whose own pipelines use R mclust and Leiden, respectively; HiSTaR with R mclust is reported in the reproduction section); Leiden or $k$-means for SpaceFlow, NichePCA, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST; and the internal model for SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace. \emph{Matched clustering} (primary analysis): every embedding clustered with the same tied-covariance Gaussian mixture
(the mclust EEE model) from 20 random starts, keeping the fit with the highest log-likelihood (label-free). SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace output no embedding and keep their own clustering under both protocols.
\emph{Initialisation experiment}: one seed-0 embedding per section and method, refitted 20 times with a single random start
each (\lotNMethods{} methods and baselines that output an embedding). \emph{R mclust experiment}: R mclust 6.0.1
(\texttt{Mclust(Z, G = k, modelNames = "EEE")}) on the same embeddings, (i) with its default initialisation under ten R random
seeds and fixed row order, (ii) initialised on all observations (\texttt{initialization = list(subset = 1:n)}), and (iii) the
same after adding Gaussian noise with 1\% of each feature's standard deviation, five times.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}
ARI against the reference annotation (manual for DLPFC and breast cancer; for STARmap, MERFISH and Stereo-seq see Limitations) is the primary metric; NMI gave a nearly identical ranking of methods (Spearman $\rho=\rhoNmiAri$ between method means; Supplementary Table~\STnmi), and
further agreement, spatial and label-free metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics{} (definitions in Supplementary
Note~\SNmetrics). For every method and section we average over seeds. Sections are not independent (the 12 DLPFC sections come
from three donors; the STARmap, MERFISH and Stereo-seq sections come from one study each), so the unit of analysis is the group:
\statClusters{} groups (three DLPFC donors, STARmap, MERFISH, breast cancer and Stereo-seq). Five of the seven groups are brain
tissue, so the overall comparison weights brain data most; we therefore also report each dataset separately. For each method we estimate the difference in mean ARI from
linear diffusion, the reference, which was chosen after the means were seen, so these comparisons are exploratory. Intervals
come from a hierarchical bootstrap (\statBoot{} resamples of groups, then of sections within a group; the estimate is the
equal-weight mean of group means) with 95\% percentile intervals (Supplementary Note~\SNstats). A method is called equivalent
to the reference if the 90\% interval lies within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI, about the seed-to-seed standard deviation. Ranks and the
probability of ranking first come from a separate loop of the same design. The analysis is repeated without the development sections (DLPFC donor 1
and the first section of STARmap and MERFISH). With \statClusters{} groups the intervals are wide; we report
uncorrected 95\% intervals and, as a check, Bonferroni-corrected intervals over the 16 comparisons (from a separate bootstrap of 20000 resamples; ranks come from their own loop of 5000), and interpret only consistent
patterns. Run time and peak memory were measured in a standardised runner that
gives every method the same input (process-tree sampling, one CPU thread per job; Supplementary Note~\SNcompute); its accuracy
agrees with the published pipelines to within the differences listed in Supplementary Table~\STagree. For the first three datasets
the accuracy analyses use the published pipelines; for breast cancer and Stereo-seq they use the runner, which records both the
method's own clustering and matched clustering.

\subsection{Simulation and whole-brain data}
Both datasets were run in the standardised runner with every method's code and settings unchanged, three seeds per method and the true
number of domains, on a shared server (125 GB RAM, CPU, one thread per job) with a cap of 30 GB per job; their run times are not compared
with the rest. They are reported separately from the main analysis and do not enter its bootstrap. For the simulation, the design, the
primary contrast and the decision rules were committed before any simulated tissue existed (Supplementary Note~\SNnewdata). The primary
contrast is $\Delta$, the ARI of linear diffusion minus that of PCA, each with its default 3-start mixture, averaged over seeds; per setting
we report its mean over the five tissues with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval over tissues. H1: $\Delta$ is larger for $m=1$ than for
$m=7$ and does not increase with $m$. H2: $\Delta$ is smallest for $w=3$, lower than for $m=7$, and does not decrease with $w$. Each needs a
difference above 0.03 ARI with an interval excluding zero. On the whole-brain sections, five findings of the main analysis were re-tested
with the same protocols on the seed-0 embeddings, with a verdict rule fixed in advance: with $S_0$ the value in the main analysis and $n_0$ a
null value (0.03 ARI for the two ranges, 0 otherwise), a finding \emph{replicates} if the new value lies on the same side of $n_0$ as $S_0$ with
a 95\% interval (bootstrap over sections) excluding $n_0$, \emph{reverses} if it lies on the other side with the interval excluding $n_0$, and
\emph{weakens} otherwise.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Linear diffusion is among the most accurate methods on brain tissue}
\emph{Claim tested (C1, first part): when every embedding is clustered in the same way, a training-free smoothing baseline is as accurate as
most published methods.}
Every method completed every section it applies to (all \nSections{} sections; BayesSpace, the 13 Visium sections), including the 17 jobs that first exceeded the memory cap, so the comparisons below have no missing cells (Supplementary Table~\STcov).
With matched clustering, linear diffusion had a mean ARI of \meanLinDlpfc, \meanLinStarmap{} and \meanLinMerfish{} on DLPFC,
STARmap and MERFISH, \meanLinBrca{} on breast cancer and \meanLinStereo{} on Stereo-seq (\figref{fig:accuracy}A; per-dataset
values for both protocols in Supplementary Table~\STari). Across all methods and both protocols, the highest dataset means were
reached by \bestDlpfc{} on DLPFC (\bestDlpfcVal), \bestStarmap{} on STARmap (\bestStarmapVal), \bestMerfish{} on MERFISH
(\bestMerfishVal), \bestBrca{} on breast cancer (\bestBrcaVal) and \bestStereo{} on Stereo-seq (\bestStereoVal). Linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than
\nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators (95\% interval above zero; \figref{fig:accuracy}B; Supplementary Table~\STstatsrob), and
this held for \nRobBetterTest{} of them without the development sections. The baseline was ahead of \listRobBetter{} (all but PCA are published methods, so 7 of the 14 published entries; BayesSpace was run on four groups only and its lower limit is very close to zero). The other
\nRobUnres{} comparisons were unresolved (\listRobUnres; for example BASS $\Delta$ \robDBass, 95\% interval \robLoBass{} to
\robHiBass, and BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$ \robDBanksyHi, \robLoBanksyHi{} to \robHiBanksyHi), and no comparator was equivalent to the
baseline within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI. With a Bonferroni correction only \nRobBetterBonf{} comparisons (\listRobBetterBonf)
remained above zero (the lower limits of PCA, and of SpaceFlow just below zero, are within 0.01 of zero, so this count can vary between two and four with the resampling seed). Ranked on all \rankSections{} sections, BASS was first in \pBestBass{} of the
bootstrap resamples, linear diffusion in \pBestLin{} and no other method in more than \pBestThirdVal; \nTopRank{} methods had rank 1 within
their 95\% interval (\figref{fig:accuracy}C; Supplementary Table~\STranks). Leaving out one dataset at a time (STARmap, MERFISH, breast cancer or Stereo-seq; the DLPFC donors count as three groups and were always kept), the baseline remained ahead of \nDropMin{} to \nDropMax{} of the 16 comparators (\nDropStarmap, \nDropMerfish, \nDropBrca{} and \nDropStereo{} respectively), against \nRobBetter{} with all datasets. Taking all datasets from the standardised
runner instead of the published pipelines gave a similar picture, with a different verdict only for \listRunnerFlip{} (\nRobBetterRunner{} comparisons favouring the baseline,
\nRobUnresRunner{} unresolved; first rank BASS \pBestBassRunner, linear diffusion \pBestLinRunner; Supplementary
Table~\STstatsrunner). With each method's default clustering \nStdBetter{}
of \nStdTot{} comparisons favoured the baseline and \nStdUnres{} (\listStdUnres) were unresolved (Supplementary
Table~\STstats). \emph{Evidence for C1:} under a common clustering protocol the baseline was more accurate than \nRobBetter{} of
\nRobTot{} comparators and could not be separated from the other \nRobUnres; these data do not show that it is better than the best
published methods, and BASS ranked first more often.

\subsection{The lead of the baseline depends on the tissue}
\emph{Claim tested (C1, second part): the baseline's lead is specific to brain tissue.}
With matched clustering, no comparator had a higher mean ARI than linear
diffusion on DLPFC, \nAheadStarmap{} on STARmap and \nAheadMerfish{} on MERFISH (BASS in both cases; BASS was also ahead in an
equal-weight average of the three brain datasets, \overallBass{} against \overallLin), but \nAheadBrca{} of \nCmpBrca{} comparators were ahead on breast
cancer and \nAheadStereo{} of \nCmpStereo{} on Stereo-seq (some by less than 0.01). Averaged over comparators, linear diffusion exceeded the
comparators by \dBrain{} ARI on the three brain datasets and trailed them by \dOtherAbs{} on the other two. On these two datasets the methods were close to each
other (\figref{fig:accuracy}A), and PCA without any smoothing was also ahead of linear diffusion (\meanPcaBrca{} against
\meanLinBrca{} on breast cancer, \meanPcaStereo{} against \meanLinStereo{} on Stereo-seq): smoothing with the settings fixed on
DLPFC did not help on these tissues, and most methods lay within a narrow range. The breast cancer annotation has 20 numbered regions of four classes; scored against the four
classes with $K=4$, every method with an embedding reached a low ARI (\brcaCoarseLo--\brcaCoarseHi; \brcaCoarseNear{} of \brcaCoarseN{} within 0.18--0.21) and the order changed (\brcaCoarseTop{} highest;
linear diffusion \brcaCoarseLin; Supplementary Table~\STbrca). On the largest Stereo-seq section (18,408 bins) BASS, GraphST,
NichePCA, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST needed \peakBigMin--\peakBigMax{} GB of RAM (BASS \peakBigBass, NichePCA \peakBigNichepca, GraphST
\peakBigGraphst, Spatial-MGCN \peakBigMgcn, DeepST \peakBigDeepst{} GB; measured on the larger server with 20 threads, where DeepST and NichePCA used several cores), reaching or exceeding the \memCap{} GB cap of the benchmark machine on the first attempt. GraphST, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST build dense graphs or matrices over all bins; for NichePCA the demand follows from its default workflow, which also has a sparse option that we did not use. Restricting the comparison to the datasets whose annotation is not known to be derived from clustering (dropping STARmap,
whose annotation provenance is unknown, and Stereo-seq, whose annotation was derived partly by clustering) gave the same picture:
\nRobBetterExpert{} comparisons favoured the baseline and \nRobUnresExpert{} were unresolved (the verdict changed for \listExpertFlip{} compared with the main analysis); BASS and linear diffusion each ranked
first in about \pBestBassExpert{} and \pBestLinExpert{} of the resamples (Supplementary Table~\STstatsexpert). On the three brain datasets alone, the baseline was ahead of \nRobBetterBrain{} of \nRobTotBrain{} comparators, and BASS and linear diffusion ranked first in \pBestBassBrain{} and \pBestLinBrain{} of the resamples (Supplementary Table~\STstatsbrain). Example partitions are
shown in \figref{fig:maps} and, for breast cancer and Stereo-seq, in Supplementary Figure~\SFmapsnew.

The edge-preserving variant AnisoST did not improve on linear diffusion overall (MERFISH: 0.363 vs 0.497), although on DLPFC it
was more accurate on spots at layer boundaries (by 0.025, 9 of 12 sections, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.034$, uncorrected;
Supplementary Figure~\SFboundary); at single-cell resolution neighbouring cells often differ by cell type, so an edge-preserving
rule blocks the smoothing needed to reveal regions. \emph{Evidence for C1:} the baseline's overall lead rested on the three brain
datasets, where it exceeded the average comparator by \dBrain{} ARI; on breast cancer and Stereo-seq it trailed by \dOtherAbs, and
\nAheadBrca{} of \nCmpBrca{} and \nAheadStereo{} of \nCmpStereo{} comparators were ahead of it. Why this happens is tested below (C4).

\subsection{Number of domains, seed variability and cost}
\emph{Scope of C1: the comparisons above give every method the annotated number of domains and average over training seeds; here we
check how far they depend on these choices.}

Every method was given the annotated number of domains $K$. Changing $K$ by one changed ARI by \kOneAvg{} on average (\kOneMean{} in the
worse direction, up to \kOneMax{}; Supplementary Table~\STk). BIC did not provide a usable label-free choice: within $K\pm2$ it selected the largest value in
\kBicEdge{} of \kN{} embeddings. At that choice the ranking of methods was largely preserved (Spearman $\rho=\kRankRho$ between method
means), but \kAboveLinBic{} moved ahead of linear diffusion (\kLinBic, rank \kLinBicRank), so the lead of the baseline
depends on giving every method the true $K$.

Seed-to-seed variability differed between methods: HiSTaR's standard deviation of ARI was \sdHistarDlpfc{} on DLPFC and \sdHistarStarmap{} on
STARmap, against \sdBaseLo--\sdBaseHi{} for the training-free baselines (Supplementary Table~\STseeds). The median time per job on one CPU
thread ranged from under 30 s for the baselines and BANKSY to about \timeMinCcst{} min for CCST, and CCST took more than 5 h on the largest
Stereo-seq section (Supplementary Table~\STres). \emph{Consequence for C1:} the baseline's lead holds when the true number of domains is
given; with a label-free choice of $K$, \kAboveLinBic{} moved ahead of it (rank \kLinBicRank), so C1 is a statement about known $K$.

\subsection{Random initialisation of the clustering step changes ARI on a fixed embedding}
\emph{Claim tested (C2, first part): with the embedding held fixed, the random start of the clustering step alone changes ARI by more
than the differences usually reported between methods.}
\figref{fig:robust}A shows the ARI of 20 single-start fits of the same mixture model on each fixed embedding. Across the
\lotN{} embeddings of \lotNMethods{} methods and baselines, the ARI range within an embedding was \lotMean{} on average and reached
\lotMax{} (\lotWorstMethod, \lotWorstSection). The training-free baselines were not immune: the mean range was \lotMeanLin{} for
linear diffusion, against \lotLo{} for \lotLoName{} and \lotHi{} for \lotHiName{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery). The best of
the 20 fits by ARI exceeded the average single fit by \lotOracle: choosing a clustering run by its agreement with the labels,
explicitly or implicitly by trying several seeds, would inflate a result by more than the few hundredths typically reported
between successive methods. Every fit for three representative methods is shown in Supplementary Figure~\SFlottery. \emph{Evidence for C2:} across \lotN{}
fixed embeddings the mean range of \lotMean{} ARI, and the gain of \lotOracle{} from picking the best fit by the labels, exceed the few
hundredths that separate successive published methods.

\subsection{The default fit of R mclust depends on its random seed}
\emph{Claim tested (C2, second part): the clustering step of GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR, R mclust with a fixed seed, gives a result that
depends on that seed.}
For data with more than 2000 observations, R mclust initialises the EM algorithm by hierarchical agglomeration on a random subset
of 2000 observations drawn with R's random number generator, so its default fit depends on the R seed. The published pipelines fix
this seed (2020 in STAGATE and GraphST, 2023 in SEDR), which makes their result reproducible but tied to an arbitrary choice. Across
the \mcsNBig{} embeddings with more than 2000 spots or cells, changing only the R seed changed ARI by \mcsSeedMeanBig{} on average
(up to \mcsSeedMax; \figref{fig:robust}B), and the range exceeded 0.1 for \mcsSeedGt\% of them; the STARmap sections, with about
1050 cells, were initialised on all cells and did not vary. Initialising on all observations makes the fit deterministic, but it remained sensitive to noise far below
the measurement noise: adding 1\% noise changed ARI by \mcsJitMean{} on average (up to \mcsJitMax; on the 18,408-bin Stereo-seq section, where one
full-data fit takes about \mcsFullMinBig{} min, only for \mcsJitNBig{} embeddings). Among embeddings with more than 2000 observations, the full-data fit
fell outside the range of the ten seeded fits for \mcsFullOutside\% (Supplementary Table~\STmclust). \emph{Evidence for C2:} a
number published from these pipelines is one draw from a distribution whose range was \mcsSeedMeanBig{} ARI on average and above 0.1
for \mcsSeedGt\% of the embeddings.

\subsection{Selecting fits by likelihood helps on cortex but not on the other tissues}
\emph{Claim tested (C3): choosing the clustering with the highest likelihood is not a general remedy for the variance shown above.}
A natural fix is to run many starts and keep the most likely fit. On the same embedding and model, the best-likelihood fit beat the
average single start by more than 0.005 ARI for \lotBLUp{} of \lotNMethods{} methods and was worse for \lotBLDown{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery).
Replacing each method's default clustering by matched clustering changed mean ARI by more than 0.005 upwards for \nGainPos{} of
\nGainTot{} methods and downwards for \nGainNeg{} (\figref{fig:matched}; Supplementary Table~\STgain); for example HiSTaR gained
\gainHistar{} (\gainPHistar) and NichePCA \gainNichepca{} (\gainPNichepca), whereas SEDR changed by \gainSedr{} (\gainPSedr) and CCST
by \gainCcst{} (\gainPCcst), all uncorrected. The reason is visible in \figref{fig:robust}C: likelihood and ARI were positively
related on DLPFC (median Spearman $\rho$ \rhoDlpfcMed) and STARmap (\rhoStarmapMed) but not on MERFISH (median $\rho$
\rhoMerfishMed; negative for \rhoNegMerfish{} of \lotNMethods{} methods), breast cancer (\rhoBrcaMed) or Stereo-seq
(\rhoStereoMed). On MERFISH the best-fitting mixture may separate cell types rather than anatomical regions.
Better optimisation of the mixture model is therefore not the same as better domains, and the clustering protocol alone can
reorder methods. \emph{Evidence for C3:} likelihood and ARI were positively related only on the two cortex datasets, and matched
clustering raised mean ARI by more than 0.005 for \nGainPos{} of \nGainTot{} methods and lowered it for \nGainNeg.

\subsection{The benefit of smoothing depends on domain size}
\emph{Claim tested (C4): the benefit of smoothing depends on the size and arrangement of the domains.} The tissue dependence above
suggests that smoothing helps where domains are large and contiguous. The pre-registered simulation tests this with the
expression differences between domains held fixed and only their geometry varied (\figref{fig:newdata}B,C; Supplementary Table~\STsim).
With layered bands, the advantage of linear diffusion over PCA fell from \simDAone{} ARI (95\% interval \simDAoneLo{} to \simDAoneHi) for bands
20 spots wide to \simDAseven{} (\simDAsevenLo{} to \simDAsevenHi) for bands about three spots wide (difference \simHone, \simHoneLo{} to
\simHoneHi; H1 \simHoneVerdict). With random blocks of $3\times3$ spots, smoothing was harmful: linear diffusion reached \simLinBthree{}
against \simPcaBthree{} for PCA ($\Delta$ \simDBthree, \simDBthreeLo{} to \simDBthreeHi; difference from the narrowest bands \simHtwo,
\simHtwoLo{} to \simHtwoHi; H2 \simHtwoVerdict). $\Delta$ rose with block size, was indistinguishable from zero at $w=8$ (\simDBeight,
\simDBeightLo{} to \simDBeightHi) and reached \simDBtwenty{} for $w=20$. PCA, which ignores location, stayed between \simPcaLo{} and \simPcaHi{}
in every setting. In an exploratory comparison, the edge-preserving AnisoST lost little on the smallest blocks (\simAnisoBthree{} at $w=3$),
whereas on the real single-cell data it was less accurate than linear diffusion; in the simulation every spot carries its domain's mean
profile, without the cell-type mixing of single-cell data. So far the simulation covers \simNMethods{} methods (\simNRuns{} runs); the
others have not yet been run on it. \emph{Evidence for C4:} both hypotheses fixed before the data existed were supported, so the
domain-size explanation rests on a controlled experiment, within the limits of one generator and the methods run so far.

\subsection{Whole-brain MERFISH: atlas-derived domains}
\emph{Claims tested (C4 and C2 on new real data): smoothing should help on large anatomical divisions, and the clustering step should
vary as before.} On the three whole-brain MERFISH sections, whose domains come from registration to the CCF rather than from clustering, \alNOk{} of the
\alNJobs{} planned runs finished (\figref{fig:newdata}D; Supplementary Table~\STallen). Among the \alNComplete{} methods that finished every
section, the highest mean ARIs with matched clustering were those of STAGATE (\alStagate), linear diffusion (\alLin), BASS
(\alBass) and SpaceFlow (\alSpaceflow), against \alPca{} for PCA: on these large anatomical divisions smoothing raised ARI by about 0.28, in line with
the simulation. With each method's own clustering, SpaceFlow (\alSpaceflowDef) and NichePCA (\alNichepcaDef), which use Leiden, were ahead of
linear diffusion (\alLinDef), whereas STAGATE fell to \alStagateDef{} with its R mclust step. AnisoST was again much less accurate than linear
diffusion (\alAniso), as on the hypothalamus MERFISH data. Several methods did not finish: \alNoneDone{} produced no result (SEDR: the R mclust
error that GraphST and STAGATE had shown on this server, left unchanged; SpaGCN: its length-scale search returned no value; Spatial-MGCN: the
30 GB cap), and four methods finished only part of their nine runs (\alPartial), because of the memory cap or of stops by the shared server's memory guard (Supplementary
Note~\SNnewdata). Missing runs are reported as such and not imputed, so this comparison is descriptive.

The findings on the clustering step were re-tested on the same sections (\gThreeNMethods{} methods with an embedding). The ARI range over
20 single-start fits of one embedding (\gThreeA, 95\% interval \gThreeALo{} to \gThreeAHi; main analysis \gThreeARef) and the dependence
of R mclust's default fit on its seed (range \gThreeB, \gThreeBLo{} to \gThreeBHi; main analysis \gThreeBRef) both replicated. Matched
clustering, which gained \gThreeCRef{} on average in the main analysis, was slightly worse than the methods' own clustering here (\gThreeC,

(\alLin{} against \alPca), as C4 predicts, and both sources of clustering variance replicated; the gain from matched clustering, part of
C3, did not.

\subsection{HiSTaR's published DLPFC accuracy was not reproduced}
\emph{Claim tested (C5, first part): published accuracy may not be reproduced from released code with its defaults.}
With our PyTorch port of the official code and its published defaults, HiSTaR reached a median ARI over the twelve DLPFC sections of
0.509 (mean 0.491, 95\% bootstrap interval over sections 0.454--0.525) with the 3-start GMM, and 0.449 (mean, seed 0)
when its embedding was clustered with R mclust as in the official pipeline, compared with a reported
median of 0.65 over the twelve sections and 0.718 on section 151673 \citep{yu2025histar}. Fixing a bug that applies random masking at inference did not change accuracy (0.487; Supplementary Table~\SThistar).
Possible causes include per-section tuning of the loss weights (described in the HiSTaR supplement), unpublished preprocessing,
the shared PCA input of this benchmark (our port received the same principal components as the other methods) and the choice of
seed; the results above show that the clustering step alone can produce per-section differences of this size.
We report these numbers to document what our port of the released code and its defaults reproduce (we did not run the official code), not to question the method itself. \emph{Evidence for C5:} with
the published defaults, our port reached a median DLPFC ARI of 0.509 against a reported 0.65.

\subsection{Released code of one method selects the reported epoch using the labels}
\emph{Claim tested (C5, second part): released code can use the annotation to select the reported result.}
Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts (\texttt{DLPFC\_test.py}, \texttt{HBC\_test.py}) compute ARI against the annotation at every
training epoch and keep the best epoch for reporting. Applying the same selection rule in our runs (seed 0, \mgcnLeakN{} sections),
this label-selected epoch had a mean ARI of \mgcnOracleDlpfc{} on DLPFC, \mgcnOracleStarmap{} on STARmap and \mgcnOracleMerfish{}
on MERFISH, against \mgcnFinalDlpfc, \mgcnFinalStarmap{} and \mgcnFinalMerfish{} for the final epoch used in our benchmark, an
inflation of \mgcnLeakDlpfc{} on DLPFC, the dataset the released scripts target, and \mgcnLeak{} on average over all three datasets (\mgcnLeakStarmap{} on STARmap, where we applied the rule ourselves; Supplementary Table~\STepoch). We do not know whether the published numbers were obtained with these scripts; we report what the released code does. Selecting a run, epoch or seed by
agreement with the labels is a form of label leakage: on DLPFC it makes the method look competitive with the training-free
baselines (\mgcnOracleDlpfc{} against \meanLinDlpfc) when its label-free result is not. On the breast cancer section, for which
the released repository provides a configuration, the inflation was smaller (\mgcnLeakBrca{} ARI over \mgcnLeakBrcaN{} seeds), but that is a tuned configuration that can carry its own label influence. An automated screen of the released
repositories (11 benchmarked methods and three further spatial methods) for other patterns of label use, with every candidate line read by hand, is reported in Supplementary
Table~\STaudit{} and Supplementary Note~\SNaudit. \emph{Evidence for C5:} Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts select the reported
epoch by ARI against the labels, which raised its DLPFC ARI by \mgcnLeakDlpfc{} in our runs, and the screen found no other case of a result chosen by agreement with the labels.

\section{Discussion}
We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines on \nSections{} sections from four
technologies and three tissue types and separated the learned embedding from the clustering step. When all embeddings were
clustered in the same way, linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than \nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators and
could not be separated from the rest; the intervals were too wide to show equivalence with any method, and BASS ranked first more often (\pBestBass{} against \pBestLin) in the primary analysis; the order reversed in the runner sensitivity analysis and the intervals overlap. This lead
came from the brain datasets: on breast cancer and mouse embryo the baseline was slightly behind most methods, including PCA without
smoothing, and the methods were close to each other. The clustering step was a larger source of
variation than most differences between methods: random initialisation of the same mixture model spanned \lotMean{} ARI on a fixed
embedding, the default fit of R mclust, the final step of GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR, depended on a fixed but arbitrary random seed, and
better likelihood did not reliably mean better domains. Reproducibility was limited both by the clustering step and by choices in
released code, including selecting a reported epoch with the labels. A pre-registered simulation showed that the advantage of
smoothing depends on domain size, and on whole-brain MERFISH sections with atlas-derived domains the baseline was again among the most
accurate methods, while the variance of the clustering step replicated.

Why does simple smoothing do so well on brain tissue? On layered tissue such as cortex, domains are large, contiguous regions, and
averaging each spot with its neighbours removes most of the sampling noise that separates spots of the same layer; much of what a
graph encoder learns may be this smoothing. In the breast cancer section and the embryo sections the methods performed similarly,
and smoothing gave no gain over PCA alone. The simulation supports one explanation: with the expression differences between
domains held fixed, the gain from smoothing fell steadily as the domains became narrower and turned into a large loss for small, interleaved
patches, and on the whole-brain sections, whose anatomical divisions are large, smoothing again gave a large gain over PCA. Domains in the
breast cancer and embryo sections may be smaller, interleaved and defined partly by cell composition, so that a fixed amount of smoothing
tuned on cortex blurs them. The simulation does not exclude other explanations: the annotation itself may have been derived partly from
expression clustering (the MOSTA ``Cavity'' class and the Stereo-seq tissues), which would favour methods similar to that clustering; and on the square bin grid the
six-nearest-neighbour graph has ties and a different physical scale. Neighbourhood-composition methods such as CellCharter
\citep{varrone2024cellcharter} and MENDER \citep{yuan2024mender} take a related smoothing-like view. The brain/other contrast also rests on one breast cancer
section and three embryo sections of one study, and most published defaults were set on DLPFC or similar brain data too, so the pattern is not specific to the
baseline. The ranking of methods is therefore tissue-dependent, and a benchmark on one tissue, especially DLPFC alone, does not identify a generally best
method. The reference annotation of breast cancer is histological and mostly not recoverable from expression (ARI \brcaCoarseLo--\brcaCoarseHi{} against its four classes), so
differences of a few hundredths on that one section may be close to noise. On single-cell MERFISH data, neighbouring cells often belong to different cell types, and the
best-fitting mixture can then describe cell types rather than regions, which may explain why likelihood and ARI disagree there. For a
practitioner, the results suggest including a smoothing baseline as a reference, choosing the clustering step deliberately and reporting its
variance. These results agree with recent benchmarks \citep{explanatory2026,hu2024gbbench,nar2025bench} and add a direct measurement
of clustering variance on fixed embeddings for \lotNMethods{} methods across four technologies. For the choice of method, the
simulation suggests a rule of thumb: smoothing with a fixed neighbourhood helps when domains are many spots wide and hurts when they are
only a few spots wide, where in the simulation an edge-preserving rule or no smoothing did better.

\paragraph{Recommendations.}
(1) Report the clustering protocol: algorithm, implementation and version, initialisation, number of starts, random seeds and how
they were chosen (\figref{fig:robust}A,B).
(2) Never select seeds, epochs or runs by agreement with the labels (Supplementary Table~\STepoch).
(3) Report distributions over training seeds and over clustering runs or R seeds, with per-section values (Supplementary
Tables~\STseeds--\STmclust).
(4) Compare encoders under the same clustering, include training-free baselines such as neighbourhood smoothing, and evaluate on
more than one tissue (\figref{fig:accuracy}A, \figref{fig:matched}).
(5) Report run time, memory and seed variance alongside accuracy (Supplementary Table~\STres).
(6) Release per-run results and seeds so that variance can be recomputed.
(7) If fits are selected, use a label-free criterion and report how often it agrees with the annotation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All methods were run with default or published settings, without a tuning budget, so the results do not show what each method can
reach with its authors' own tuning (we could not reproduce HiSTaR's per-section tuning). The reference baseline was chosen after
the means were seen, and the comparisons with it are exploratory. Four methods (and GraphST on DLPFC) were run with one seed in the published pipelines
and BayesSpace on Visium only (Supplementary Table~\STcov), so seed variance on the first three datasets is unavailable for them.
Five methods reached or exceeded the \memCap{} GB cap of the benchmark machine on the largest Stereo-seq section (up to \peakBigMax{} GB) and
were rerun on a server with 125 GB RAM; those runs used a different CPU, so their run times are not compared with the rest, and the
memory figures describe these implementations on this section, not the methods in general. NichePCA's default workflow aggregates
all genes before PCA; its package also offers other orders of these steps. The MOSTA annotation includes a ``Cavity'' class that covers \cavityFracLo--\cavityFracHi\% of the bins of the three sections and has a median UMI count per bin of \cavityRatioLo--\cavityRatioHi{} times that of the other bins, so this class may partly reflect low count depth. Settings were the same on every dataset except where a tutorial prescribes technology-specific values (GraphST's Stereo-seq mode; BASS's gene selection and cluster number, set on DLPFC and reused on breast cancer and Stereo-seq; Supplementary Table~\STmethods); other dataset-specific tutorial settings (for example SEDR's Stereo-seq graph or Spatial-MGCN's configuration for this breast
cancer section) were not used, and neighbourhood radii were expressed in spot or bin spacings, which on Stereo-seq bin50 data
covers a smaller physical area than on Visium; on the square bin grid a six-nearest-neighbour graph also has ties among equidistant
bins. The initialisation and R
mclust experiments use one seed-0 embedding per section and method and do not include training-seed variance. Three of the five
datasets are brain tissue, and the non-brain data comprise one breast cancer section and three embryo sections of one study;
Slide-seq, Xenium and Visium HD data are not covered, and sections with more than about 20,000 cells only through the three whole-brain
MERFISH sections, on which several methods did not finish within 30 GB or failed (Supplementary Table~\STallen); those results are
descriptive and outside the main bootstrap. The simulation uses one generator (independent negative-binomial counts with fixed domain
profiles, without cell-type mixing or spatial autocorrelation within a domain), and so far covers \simNMethods{} of the 16 comparators
and the reference; the deep methods have not been run on it.
The STARmap and MERFISH annotations were distributed with the BASS analyses, and BASS was among the most accurate methods on these
data. The MOSTA tissue annotation was derived partly from clustering of the expression data and then curated, which may favour
methods similar to that clustering, and the breast cancer annotation was distributed with the SEDR analyses; we did not verify
either annotation independently. The 10x breast cancer and MOSTA data are also the showcase data of several benchmarked methods, and BASS selects its own genes, which may be part of its advantage; both could favour those methods. SpatialPCA and PRECAST, cited in the Introduction, were not benchmarked in this study. The number of domains was given to every method; results with a misspecified or label-free $K$ are reported in Supplementary Table~\STk. All runs used one CPU thread, except the reruns on the 125 GB server, where DeepST and NichePCA used several cores, so run times are not
representative of GPU use. ARI is the primary metric; other metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics.
