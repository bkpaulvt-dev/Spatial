\abstract{Spatial domain identification methods are usually compared by small differences in adjusted Rand index (ARI),
mostly on one tissue and one run. We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines
on \nSections{} annotated sections of brain, breast cancer and mouse embryo from 10x Visium, STARmap, MERFISH and Stereo-seq,
separating each learned embedding from its clustering step. With the same clustering for every embedding, a training-free
neighbourhood-smoothing baseline had a higher mean ARI than \nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators and could not be separated
from the other \nRobUnres{} (exploratory comparisons); BASS and the baseline were the most likely to rank first. Its lead came from
brain tissue: on breast cancer and embryo most methods, including PCA without smoothing, were slightly ahead.
On fixed embeddings, random restarts of the same Gaussian mixture changed ARI by \lotMean{} on average (up to \lotMax),
and R mclust's default fit depended on its random seed. Fit likelihood tracked ARI only on cortex. HiSTaR's published accuracy was not reproduced, and released code of one method
selects its reported epoch using the labels. We recommend reporting the clustering protocol and its variance, never selecting
runs with labels, and evaluating training-free baselines on several tissues.}

\keywords{spatial transcriptomics, spatial domains, clustering, reproducibility, benchmarking}

\section{Introduction}

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at thousands of locations in a tissue section
while retaining their coordinates. Identifying \emph{spatial domains}, regions with coherent
expression such as cortical layers, is one of the first analyses of such data, and many methods
have been proposed for it, from Bayesian and probabilistic models \citep{zhao2021bayesspace,liu2023precast},
spatially aware dimension reduction \citep{shang2022spatialpca} and neighbourhood-augmented features
\citep{singhal2024banksy,varrone2024cellcharter,yuan2024mender} to graph neural networks
\citep{hu2021spagcn,dong2022stagate,long2023graphst} and hierarchical graph variational
autoencoders such as HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar}.

Most of these methods are evaluated on the twelve manually annotated human dorsolateral prefrontal
cortex (DLPFC) sections \citep{maynard2021dlpfc} and report median adjusted Rand index (ARI) \citep{hubert1985ari}
improvements of a few hundredths over previous methods \citep{dong2022stagate,long2023graphst,yu2025histar}. Several
(for example GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR) share the same last step: the learned embedding is clustered once with the
EEE Gaussian mixture of the R package mclust \citep{scrucca2016mclust}. Benchmarks have found that rankings depend strongly
on the dataset \citep{yuan2024benchmark,nar2025bench}, that deep methods vary with the random seed \citep{hu2024gbbench}, that
replacing each method's clustering algorithm changes results \citep{nar2025bench}, and that preprocessing and clustering
choices often matter more than architectural novelty \citep{explanatory2026}. Simple training-free pipelines can also be
competitive \citep{nichepca2025,do2026graphbg}. What these studies do not measure directly is how much of the variation
comes from the clustering step itself when the embedding is held fixed.

We benchmark \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines along three axes. \emph{Accuracy}: how do the
methods compare across four technologies and three tissue types when their embeddings are clustered in the same way, and how does this change with
each method's own clustering? \emph{Robustness}: on a \emph{fixed} embedding, how much does ARI change with the random
initialisation of the mixture model and with the random seed of R mclust, and does selecting fits by likelihood help?
\emph{Reproducibility}: do published results reproduce with the released code (here HiSTaR's DLPFC results), and does
released code select models using the labels? The study design is summarised in \figref{fig:overview}. We find that a
standard neighbourhood-smoothing baseline is among the most accurate methods on brain tissue but not on the other tissues, that the clustering step varies more than
most differences between methods, and that released code can use the labels for model selection.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data}
\textbf{DLPFC (10x Visium).} Twelve sections from three donors \citep{maynard2021dlpfc}; seven
annotated layers (five for donor 2), \texttt{layer\_guess\_reordered}.
\textbf{STARmap mouse medial prefrontal cortex.} Three sections (about 1050 cells, 166 genes) with
four annotated layers (L1, L2/3, L5, L6) \citep{wang2018starmap}.
\textbf{MERFISH mouse hypothalamus.} Animal 1, five sections from Bregma $-0.04$ to $-0.24$ (about
5500 cells, 155 genes) with eight annotated regions \citep{moffitt2018merfish}. The STARmap and
MERFISH annotations are those distributed with the BASS analyses \citep{li2022bass}, one of the benchmarked methods
(see Limitations).
\textbf{Human breast cancer (10x Visium).} Block A Section 1 of the 10x Genomics demonstration data \citep{tenx2020brca}
(3798 spots), with the 20 regions (ductal and lobular carcinoma \emph{in situ}, invasive ductal carcinoma, tumour edge and healthy
tissue) of the annotation distributed with the SEDR analyses \citep{xu2024sedr}.
\textbf{Mouse embryo (Stereo-seq).} Three sections of the MOSTA atlas \citep{chen2022mosta} at bin50 resolution (about 25~\textmu m):
E9.5 E1S1 (5913 bins, 12 annotated tissues), E10.5 E2S1 (8494 bins, 18) and E10.5 E1S1 (18,408 bins, 13), with the published
tissue annotation. These two datasets were added after the analysis of the first three was complete, without changing any setting.
In total, \nSections{} sections from four technologies (Supplementary Table~\STqc; Supplementary Note~\SNdata).

\subsection{Preprocessing}
DLPFC, breast cancer and Stereo-seq (whole transcriptome; Stereo-seq bins without counts removed): genes in fewer than 50 spots or with fewer than 10 counts were removed, 2000 highly
variable genes were selected (Seurat v3 flavour), counts were library-size normalised,
log-transformed and scaled, and reduced to 200 principal components (PCs). STARmap and MERFISH
(targeted panels): all genes, same normalisation, 50 PCs. All methods received the same input
where they accept one; BANKSY used the log-normalised genes, and SpaGCN, GraphST and the other published methods
used their own documented preprocessing of the same raw counts. The true number of domains was given to every method,
and unannotated spots were excluded from evaluation.

\subsection{Methods}
Methods were chosen to cover the main families of spatial domain identification with maintained public code that runs on
CPU: deep graph encoders, statistical and neighbourhood methods, and Bayesian models. Every method was run with its official
code and default or tutorial settings on one CPU thread; none was tuned per dataset. Versions, settings and deviations from
the tutorials (for example the neighbourhood radius, expressed in spot spacings so that it transfers across technologies) are
listed in Supplementary Table~\STmethods.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep graph encoders.} HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar} (PyTorch port of the official code with the published
defaults: cross-level weight 0.3, 200+200 epochs), GraphST \citep{long2023graphst} (official package and tutorial: 600 epochs,
20-PC projection, R mclust; with and without its label refinement; on Stereo-seq its documented setting for Stereo-seq data,
\texttt{datatype='Stereo'}), SpaGCN \citep{hu2021spagcn} (tutorial without histology,
Louvain resolution search to the true number of domains), STAGATE \citep{dong2022stagate}, SEDR \citep{xu2024sedr},
SpaceFlow \citep{ren2022spaceflow}, CCST \citep{li2022ccst}, Spatial-MGCN \citep{wang2023spatialmgcn} and DeepST
\citep{xu2022deepst}. Spatial-MGCN's released test script reports the epoch with the highest ARI against the annotation;
in the benchmark we report its final epoch (label-free) and give the label-selected value separately.
\item \textbf{Statistical and neighbourhood methods.} BANKSY \citep{singhal2024banksy} (official \texttt{pybanksy},
18 neighbours, $m_{\max}=1$, 20 PCs; $\lambda=0.8$, recommended for domain segmentation, and $\lambda=0.2$, recommended for cell
typing, included for reference), NichePCA \citep{nichepca2025} (a published training-free method: neighbour aggregation and PCA),
and the Bayesian models BASS \citep{li2022bass} and BayesSpace \citep{zhao2021bayesspace}, which cluster internally. BayesSpace
requires a spot lattice and was run on the Visium sections only.
\item \textbf{Training-free baselines.} \emph{Linear diffusion} is standard neighbourhood smoothing (graph Laplacian
smoothing): the top 30 PCs are smoothed on the spatial six-nearest-neighbour graph,
$H^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha)H^{(t)}+\alpha D^{-1}AH^{(t)}$, $\alpha=0.5$, $T=10$. \emph{AnisoST} uses the same scheme with edge weights
recomputed at every step, $w_{ij}^{(t)}=\exp(-\|h_i^{(t)}-h_j^{(t)}\|^2/\kappa^{(t)})$ (Perona--Malik-type edge-preserving
diffusion \citep{perona1990}; $\kappa^{(t)}$ the median squared edge difference); a similar idea has been used inside a deep
model \citep{wang2024pearlst}. \emph{PCA} without smoothing is the non-spatial reference. All baseline settings were fixed on
DLPFC donor 1 before any other data were analysed (sensitivity: Supplementary Figure~\SFsens).
\end{itemize}
Seeds: three per section for most methods; one for STAGATE, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and BASS in the published pipelines and for
GraphST on DLPFC, and one run per section for BayesSpace (Supplementary Table~\STcov). The breast cancer and Stereo-seq sections
were run only in the standardised runner described below, with three seeds per method (two for CCST on the largest Stereo-seq
section, where one run takes more than 5 h; one run for BayesSpace; DeepST seeds 1 and 2 exceeded the memory cap on Stereo-seq
E10.5 E2S1). On these two datasets every job had a memory cap of
\memCap{} GB on a 15 GB machine and runs that exceeded it are reported as failures; the earlier runs on the first three datasets
had no cap (the largest, BayesSpace on DLPFC, used 12.5 GB).

\paragraph{Neutrality.} The authors developed AnisoST, and linear diffusion and PCA are its ablations; the authors did not
develop any of the published methods. To limit bias, all baseline settings were frozen on the four sections of DLPFC donor 1, every
published method was run with its authors' defaults, and all comparisons are repeated without the six development sections
(those four and the first STARmap and MERFISH sections, used to test the pipelines).

\subsection{Clustering protocols}
\emph{Default clustering}: the clustering step of each method's official pipeline. This is R mclust (EEE) for GraphST, STAGATE and
SEDR; a 3-start scikit-learn Gaussian mixture with tied covariance \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn} for HiSTaR, BANKSY and the three
baselines; Leiden or $k$-means for SpaceFlow, NichePCA, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and DeepST; and the internal model for SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace. \emph{Matched clustering} (primary analysis): every embedding clustered with the same tied-covariance Gaussian mixture
(the mclust EEE model) from 20 random starts, keeping the fit with the highest log-likelihood (label-free). SpaGCN, BASS and
BayesSpace output no embedding and keep their own clustering under both protocols.
\emph{Initialisation experiment}: one seed-0 embedding per section and method, refitted 20 times with a single random start
each (\lotNMethods{} methods and baselines that output an embedding). \emph{R mclust experiment}: R mclust 6.0.1
(\texttt{Mclust(Z, G = k, modelNames = "EEE")}) on the same embeddings, (i) with its default initialisation under ten R random
seeds and fixed row order, (ii) initialised on all observations (\texttt{initialization = list(subset = 1:n)}), and (iii) the
same after adding Gaussian noise with 1\% of each feature's standard deviation, five times.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}
ARI against the manual annotation is the primary metric; NMI gave a nearly identical ranking of methods (Spearman $\rho=\rhoNmiAri$ between method means; Supplementary Table~\STnmi), and
further agreement, spatial and label-free metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics{} (definitions in Supplementary
Note~\SNmetrics). For every method and section we average over seeds. Sections are not independent (the 12 DLPFC sections come
from three donors; the STARmap, MERFISH and Stereo-seq sections come from one study each), so the unit of analysis is the group:
\statClusters{} groups (three DLPFC donors, STARmap, MERFISH, breast cancer and Stereo-seq). Five of the seven groups are brain
tissue, so the overall comparison weights brain data most; we therefore also report each dataset separately. For each method we estimate the difference in mean ARI from
linear diffusion, the reference, which was chosen after the means were seen, so these comparisons are exploratory. Intervals
come from a hierarchical bootstrap (\statBoot{} resamples of groups, then of sections within a group; the estimate is the
equal-weight mean of group means) with 95\% percentile intervals (Supplementary Note~\SNstats). A method is called equivalent
to the reference if the 90\% interval lies within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI, about the seed-to-seed standard deviation. Ranks and the
probability of ranking first come from the same bootstrap. The analysis is repeated without the development sections (DLPFC donor 1
and the first section of STARmap and MERFISH). With \statClusters{} groups the intervals are wide; we report
uncorrected 95\% intervals and, as a check, Bonferroni-corrected intervals over the 16 comparisons, and interpret only consistent
patterns. Run time and peak memory were measured in a standardised runner that
gives every method the same input (process-tree sampling, one CPU thread per job; Supplementary Note~\SNcompute); its accuracy
agrees with the published pipelines to within the differences listed in Supplementary Table~\STagree. For the first three datasets
the accuracy analyses use the published pipelines; for breast cancer and Stereo-seq they use the runner, which records both the
method's own clustering and matched clustering.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Linear diffusion is among the most accurate methods on brain tissue}
With matched clustering, linear diffusion had a mean ARI of \meanLinDlpfc, \meanLinStarmap{} and \meanLinMerfish{} on DLPFC,
STARmap and MERFISH, \meanLinBrca{} on breast cancer and \meanLinStereo{} on Stereo-seq (\figref{fig:accuracy}A; per-dataset
values for both protocols in Supplementary Table~\STari). Across all methods and both protocols, the highest dataset means were
reached by \bestDlpfc{} on DLPFC (\bestDlpfcVal), \bestStarmap{} on STARmap (\bestStarmapVal), \bestMerfish{} on MERFISH
(\bestMerfishVal), \bestBrca{} on breast cancer (\bestBrcaVal) and \bestStereo{} on Stereo-seq (\bestStereoVal). Linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than
\nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators (95\% interval above zero; \figref{fig:accuracy}B; Supplementary Table~\STstatsrob), and
this held for \nRobBetterTest{} of them without the development sections. The baseline was ahead of \listRobBetter. The other
\nRobUnres{} comparisons were unresolved (\listRobUnres; for example BASS $\Delta$ \robDBass, 95\% interval \robLoBass{} to
\robHiBass, and BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$ \robDBanksyHi, \robLoBanksyHi{} to \robHiBanksyHi), and no comparator was equivalent to the
baseline within $\pm\statMargin$ ARI. With a Bonferroni correction only \nRobBetterBonf{} comparisons (\listRobBetterBonf)
remained above zero. Ranked on the \rankSections{} sections that every method completed, BASS was first in \pBestBass{} of the
bootstrap resamples, linear diffusion in \pBestLin{} and \pBestThird{} in \pBestThirdVal; \nTopRank{} methods had rank 1 within
their 95\% interval (\figref{fig:accuracy}C; Supplementary Table~\STranks). Among the \rankMethodsComplete{} methods with a result
on every section, which excludes BASS, linear diffusion was first in \pBestLinComplete. Taking all datasets from the standardised
runner instead of the published pipelines gave the same picture (\nRobBetterRunner{} comparisons favouring the baseline,
\nRobUnresRunner{} unresolved; first rank BASS \pBestBassRunner, linear diffusion \pBestLinRunner; Supplementary
Table~\STstatsrunner). With each method's default clustering \nStdBetter{}
of \nStdTot{} comparisons favoured the baseline and \nStdUnres{} (\listStdUnres) were unresolved (Supplementary
Table~\STstats).

\subsection{The lead of the baseline depends on the tissue}
With matched clustering, no comparator had a higher mean ARI than linear
diffusion on DLPFC, \nAheadStarmap{} on STARmap and \nAheadMerfish{} on MERFISH (BASS in both cases; BASS was also ahead in an
equal-weight average of the three brain datasets, \overallBass{} against \overallLin), but \nAheadBrca{} of \nCmpBrca{} comparators were ahead on breast
cancer and \nAheadStereo{} of \nCmpStereo{} on Stereo-seq. Averaged over comparators, linear diffusion exceeded the
comparators by \dBrain{} ARI on the three brain datasets and trailed them by \dOtherAbs{} on the other two. On these two datasets the methods were close to each
other (\figref{fig:accuracy}A), and PCA without any smoothing was also ahead of linear diffusion (\meanPcaBrca{} against
\meanLinBrca{} on breast cancer, \meanPcaStereo{} against \meanLinStereo{} on Stereo-seq): smoothing with the settings fixed on
DLPFC did not help on these tissues, and most methods lay within a narrow range. The breast cancer annotation has 20 numbered regions of four classes; scored against the four
classes with $K=4$, every method with an embedding reached a low ARI (\brcaCoarseLo--\brcaCoarseHi) and the order changed (\brcaCoarseTop{} highest;
linear diffusion \brcaCoarseLin; Supplementary Table~\STbrca). In our runs \listOom{} exceeded the \memCap{} GB memory cap on the
largest Stereo-seq section (18,408 bins) and have no result there; they are compared on the remaining sections. Restricting the comparison to the datasets with an expert annotation (dropping STARmap,
whose annotation provenance is unknown, and Stereo-seq, whose annotation was derived partly by clustering) gave the same picture:
\nRobBetterExpert{} comparisons favoured the baseline, \nRobUnresExpert{} were unresolved, and BASS and linear diffusion ranked
first in \pBestBassExpert{} and \pBestLinExpert{} of the resamples (Supplementary Table~\STstatsexpert). Example partitions are
shown in \figref{fig:maps} and, for breast cancer and Stereo-seq, in Supplementary Figure~\SFmapsnew.

\subsection{Number of domains, seed variability and cost}

Every method was given the annotated number of domains $K$. Changing $K$ by one changed ARI by \kOneAvg{} on average (the
larger of the two directions \kOneMean, up to \kOneMax; Supplementary Table~\STk). BIC did not provide a usable label-free choice: within $K\pm2$ it selected the largest value in
\kBicEdge{} of \kN{} embeddings. At that choice the ranking of methods was largely preserved (Spearman $\rho=\kRankRho$ between method
means), but \kAboveLinBic{} moved ahead of linear diffusion (\kLinBic, rank \kLinBicRank), so the lead of the baseline
depends on giving every method the true $K$.

Seed-to-seed variability differed between methods: HiSTaR's standard deviation of ARI was \sdHistarDlpfc{} on DLPFC and \sdHistarStarmap{} on
STARmap, against \sdBaseLo--\sdBaseHi{} for the training-free baselines (Supplementary Table~\STseeds). The median time per job on one CPU
thread ranged from under 30 s for the baselines and BANKSY to about \timeMinCcst{} min for CCST, and CCST took more than 5 h on the largest
Stereo-seq section (Supplementary Table~\STres).
The edge-preserving variant AnisoST did not improve on linear diffusion overall (MERFISH: 0.363 vs 0.497), although on DLPFC it
was more accurate on spots at layer boundaries (by 0.025, 9 of 12 sections, Wilcoxon signed-rank $p=0.034$, uncorrected;
Supplementary Figure~\SFboundary); at single-cell resolution neighbouring cells often differ by cell type, so an edge-preserving
rule blocks the smoothing needed to reveal regions.

\subsection{Random initialisation of the clustering step changes ARI on a fixed embedding}
\figref{fig:robust}A shows the ARI of 20 single-start fits of the same mixture model on each fixed embedding. Across the
\lotN{} embeddings of \lotNMethods{} methods and baselines, the ARI range within an embedding was \lotMean{} on average and reached
\lotMax{} (\lotWorstMethod, \lotWorstSection). The training-free baselines were not immune: the mean range was \lotMeanLin{} for
linear diffusion, against \lotLo{} for \lotLoName{} and \lotHi{} for \lotHiName{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery). The best of
the 20 fits by ARI exceeded the average single fit by \lotOracle: choosing a clustering run by its agreement with the labels,
explicitly or implicitly by trying several seeds, would inflate a result by more than the few hundredths typically reported
between successive methods. Every fit for three representative methods is shown in Supplementary Figure~\SFlottery.

\subsection{The default fit of R mclust depends on its random seed}
For data with more than 2000 observations, R mclust initialises the EM algorithm by hierarchical agglomeration on a random subset
of 2000 observations drawn with R's random number generator, so its default fit depends on the R seed. The published pipelines fix
this seed (2020 in STAGATE and GraphST, 2023 in SEDR), which makes their result reproducible but tied to an arbitrary choice. Across
the \mcsNBig{} embeddings with more than 2000 spots or cells, changing only the R seed changed ARI by \mcsSeedMeanBig{} on average
(up to \mcsSeedMax; \figref{fig:robust}B), and the range exceeded 0.1 for \mcsSeedGt\% of them; the STARmap sections, with about
1050 cells, were initialised on all cells and did not vary. Initialising on all observations makes the fit deterministic, but it remained sensitive to noise far below
the measurement noise: adding 1\% noise changed ARI by \mcsJitMean{} on average (up to \mcsJitMax; on the 18,408-bin Stereo-seq section, where one
full-data fit takes about 30 min, only for three embeddings). Among embeddings with more than 2000 observations, the full-data fit
fell outside the range of the ten seeded fits for \mcsFullOutside\% (Supplementary Table~\STmclust).

\subsection{Selecting fits by likelihood helps on cortex but not on the other tissues}
A natural fix is to run many starts and keep the most likely fit. On the same embedding and model, the best-likelihood fit beat the
average single start by more than 0.005 ARI for \lotBLUp{} of \lotNMethods{} methods and was worse for \lotBLDown{} (Supplementary Table~\STlottery).
Replacing each method's default clustering by matched clustering changed mean ARI by more than 0.005 upwards for \nGainPos{} of
\nGainTot{} methods and downwards for \nGainNeg{} (\figref{fig:matched}; Supplementary Table~\STgain); for example HiSTaR gained
\gainHistar{} (\gainPHistar) and NichePCA \gainNichepca{} (\gainPNichepca), whereas SEDR changed by \gainSedr{} (\gainPSedr) and CCST
by \gainCcst{} (\gainPCcst), all uncorrected. The reason is visible in \figref{fig:robust}C: likelihood and ARI were positively
related on DLPFC (median Spearman $\rho$ \rhoDlpfcMed) and STARmap (\rhoStarmapMed) but not on MERFISH (median $\rho$
\rhoMerfishMed; negative for \rhoNegMerfish{} of \lotNMethods{} methods), breast cancer (\rhoBrcaMed) or Stereo-seq
(\rhoStereoMed). On MERFISH the best-fitting mixture may separate cell types rather than anatomical regions.
Better optimisation of the mixture model is therefore not the same as better domains, and the clustering protocol alone can
reorder methods.

\subsection{HiSTaR's published DLPFC accuracy was not reproduced}
With our PyTorch port of the official code and its published defaults, HiSTaR reached a median ARI over the twelve DLPFC sections of
0.509 (mean 0.491, 95\% bootstrap interval over sections 0.454--0.525) with the 3-start GMM, and 0.449 (mean, seed 0)
when its embedding was clustered with R mclust as in the official pipeline, compared with a reported
median of 0.65 over the twelve sections and 0.718 on section 151673 \citep{yu2025histar}. Fixing a bug that applies random masking at inference did not change accuracy (0.487; Supplementary Table~\SThistar).
Possible causes include per-section tuning of the loss weights (described in the HiSTaR supplement), unpublished preprocessing,
the shared PCA input of this benchmark (our port received the same principal components as the other methods) and the choice of
seed; the results above show that the clustering step alone can produce per-section differences of this size.
We report these numbers to document what the released code and defaults reproduce, not to question the method itself.

\subsection{Released code of one method selects the reported epoch using the labels}
Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts (\texttt{DLPFC\_test.py}, \texttt{HBC\_test.py}) compute ARI against the annotation at every
training epoch and keep the best epoch for reporting. Applying the same selection rule in our runs (seed 0, \mgcnLeakN{} sections),
this label-selected epoch had a mean ARI of \mgcnOracleDlpfc{} on DLPFC, \mgcnOracleStarmap{} on STARmap and \mgcnOracleMerfish{}
on MERFISH, against \mgcnFinalDlpfc, \mgcnFinalStarmap{} and \mgcnFinalMerfish{} for the final epoch used in our benchmark, an
inflation of \mgcnLeak{} on average (\mgcnLeakStarmap{} on STARmap; Supplementary Table~\STepoch). We do not know whether the published numbers were obtained with these scripts; we report what the released code does. Selecting a run, epoch or seed by
agreement with the labels is a form of label leakage: on DLPFC it makes the method look competitive with the training-free
baselines (\mgcnOracleDlpfc{} against \meanLinDlpfc) when its label-free result is not. On the breast cancer section, for which
the released repository provides a configuration, the inflation was smaller (\mgcnLeakBrca{} ARI over \mgcnLeakBrcaN{} seeds). An automated screen of the released
repositories (11 benchmarked methods and three further spatial methods) for other patterns of label use, with every candidate line read by hand, is reported in Supplementary
Table~\STaudit{} and Supplementary Note~\SNaudit.

\section{Discussion}
We benchmarked \nMethodsPub{} published methods and three training-free baselines on \nSections{} sections from four
technologies and three tissue types and separated the learned embedding from the clustering step. When all embeddings were
clustered in the same way, linear diffusion had a higher mean ARI than \nRobBetter{} of \nRobTot{} comparators and
could not be separated from the rest; the intervals were too wide to show equivalence with any method, and BASS ranked first more often (\pBestBass{} against \pBestLin). This lead
came from the brain datasets: on breast cancer and mouse embryo the baseline was slightly behind most methods, including PCA without
smoothing, and the methods were close to each other. The clustering step was a larger source of
variation than most differences between methods: random initialisation of the same mixture model spanned \lotMean{} ARI on a fixed
embedding, the default fit of R mclust, the final step of GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR, depended on a fixed but arbitrary random seed, and
better likelihood did not reliably mean better domains. Reproducibility was limited both by the clustering step and by choices in
released code, including selecting a reported epoch with the labels.

Why does simple smoothing do so well on brain tissue? On layered tissue such as cortex, domains are large, contiguous regions, and
averaging each spot with its neighbours removes most of the sampling noise that separates spots of the same layer; much of what a
graph encoder learns may be this smoothing. In tumour and embryo sections, domains are smaller, interleaved and defined partly by
cell composition; a possible explanation, which we did not test, is that a fixed amount of smoothing tuned on cortex then blurs
them. There the methods performed similarly, and smoothing gave no gain over PCA alone. The ranking
of methods is therefore tissue-dependent, and a benchmark on one tissue, especially DLPFC alone, does not identify a generally best
method. On single-cell MERFISH data, neighbouring cells often belong to different cell types, and the
best-fitting mixture can then describe cell types rather than regions, which may explain why likelihood and ARI disagree there. For a
practitioner, the results suggest starting with a smoothing baseline, choosing the clustering step deliberately and reporting its
variance. These results agree with recent benchmarks \citep{explanatory2026,hu2024gbbench,nar2025bench} and add a direct measurement
of clustering variance on fixed embeddings for \lotNMethods{} methods across four technologies.

\paragraph{Recommendations.}
(1) Report the clustering protocol: algorithm, implementation and version, initialisation, number of starts, random seeds and how
they were chosen (\figref{fig:robust}A,B).
(2) Never select seeds, epochs or runs by agreement with the labels (Supplementary Table~\STepoch).
(3) Report distributions over training seeds and over clustering runs or R seeds, with per-section values (Supplementary
Tables~\STseeds--\STmclust).
(4) Compare encoders under the same clustering, include training-free baselines such as neighbourhood smoothing, and evaluate on
more than one tissue (\figref{fig:accuracy}A, \figref{fig:matched}).
(5) Report run time, memory and seed variance alongside accuracy (Supplementary Table~\STres).
(6) Release per-run results and seeds so that variance can be recomputed.
(7) If fits are selected, use a label-free criterion and report how often it agrees with the annotation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All methods were run with default or published settings, without a tuning budget, so the results do not show what each method can
reach with its authors' own tuning (we could not reproduce HiSTaR's per-section tuning). The reference baseline was chosen after
the means were seen, and the comparisons with it are exploratory. Four methods (and GraphST on DLPFC) were run with one seed in the published pipelines
and BayesSpace on Visium only (Supplementary Table~\STcov), so seed variance on the first three datasets is unavailable for them.
In total, \listOom{} exceeded the \memCap{} GB memory cap on the largest Stereo-seq section; with more memory or a GPU they may run, so
this is a statement about resource use, not about accuracy. NichePCA failed there with its default workflow, which
aggregates all genes before PCA; its package also offers other orders of these steps. Settings were the same on every dataset by
design; dataset-specific tutorial settings (for example SEDR's Stereo-seq graph or Spatial-MGCN's configuration for this breast
cancer section) were not used, and neighbourhood radii were expressed in spot or bin spacings, which on Stereo-seq bin50 data
covers a smaller physical area than on Visium; on the square bin grid a six-nearest-neighbour graph also has ties among equidistant
bins. The initialisation and R
mclust experiments use one seed-0 embedding per section and method and do not include training-seed variance. Three of the five
datasets are brain tissue, and the non-brain data comprise one breast cancer section and three embryo sections of one study;
Slide-seq, Xenium and Visium HD data and sections with more than about 20,000 spots are not covered, and no simulated data were used.
The STARmap and MERFISH annotations were distributed with the BASS analyses, and BASS was among the most accurate methods on these
data. The MOSTA tissue annotation was derived partly from clustering of the expression data and then curated, which may favour
methods similar to that clustering, and the breast cancer annotation was distributed with the SEDR analyses; we did not verify
either annotation independently. The number of domains was given to every method; results with a misspecified or label-free $K$ are reported in Supplementary Table~\STk. All runs used one CPU thread, so run times are not
representative of GPU use. ARI is the primary metric; other metrics are in Supplementary Table~\STmetrics.
