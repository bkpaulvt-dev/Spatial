\abstract{\textbf{Motivation:} New methods for spatial domain identification are usually
compared by small differences in adjusted Rand index (ARI), mostly on the twelve human DLPFC
Visium sections. Most of them obtain domains by clustering a learned embedding, typically with a
single run of a Gaussian mixture model (mclust, EEE). How much of the reported differences is due to
the encoder and how much to this final clustering step is rarely measured.\\
\textbf{Results:} We evaluated \nMethodsPub{} published methods (HiSTaR, GraphST, BANKSY, SpaGCN, STAGATE,
SEDR, SpaceFlow, NichePCA, CCST, Spatial-MGCN, DeepST, BASS and BayesSpace) and three training-free
graph-diffusion baselines on \nSections{} annotated sections from three technologies (10x Visium,
STARmap, MERFISH). With its official code, HiSTaR reached a median ARI of 0.51 on DLPFC instead of
the reported 0.65. On \lotN{} fixed embeddings from \lotNMethods{} methods, refitting the same mixture
model from different random starts changed ARI by \lotMean{} on average (\lotLo--\lotHi{} depending on
the method) and by up to \lotMax; selecting the best of 20 fits by ARI would inflate results by
\lotOracle. R mclust is deterministic for a given input, but merely permuting the order of the
spots changed its ARI by up to \mcPermMax, and adding noise at 1\% of the feature scale by up to
\mcJitMax. Selecting fits by likelihood did not resolve this: matched 20-start clustering helped
\nGainPos{} of \nGainTot{} methods and hurt \nGainNeg. The highest mean ARI was reached by
\bestDlpfc{} on DLPFC, \bestStarmap{} on STARmap and \bestMerfish{} on MERFISH; the training-free
linear diffusion baseline was not significantly different from the best methods and
significantly better than \nPooledSig{} of \nPooled{} comparisons. In addition, the released code
of one method selects its reported epoch by agreement with the labels, which raised its mean ARI by
\mgcnLeak. We give reporting recommendations for benchmarks.\\
\textbf{Availability and implementation:} Code, per-run results and figures: \todo{GitHub URL and
Zenodo DOI}. All public data sources are listed in the Data availability section.}

\section{Introduction}

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene expression at thousands of locations in a tissue section
while retaining their coordinates. Identifying \emph{spatial domains}, regions with coherent
expression such as cortical layers, is one of the first analyses of such data, and many methods
have been proposed for it, from Bayesian models \citep{zhao2021bayesspace} and
neighbourhood-augmented features \citep{singhal2024banksy} to graph neural networks
\citep{hu2021spagcn,dong2022stagate,long2023graphst} and hierarchical graph variational
autoencoders such as HiSTaR \citep{yu2025histar}.

Most of these methods are evaluated on the twelve manually annotated human dorsolateral prefrontal
cortex (DLPFC) sections \citep{maynard2021dlpfc} and report median ARI improvements of a few
hundredths over previous methods. Many share the same last step: the learned embedding is clustered
once with mclust's EEE Gaussian mixture \citep{scrucca2016mclust}. Benchmarks have found that
rankings depend strongly on the dataset \citep{yuan2024benchmark,nar2025bench}, that deep methods
vary with the random seed \citep{nar2025bench}, that replacing each method's clustering algorithm
changes results \citep{hu2024gbbench}, and that preprocessing and clustering choices often matter
more than architectural novelty \citep{explanatory2026}. Simple training-free pipelines are also
competitive \citep{nichepca2025,do2026graphbg}.

We complement these studies with a direct measurement of the variance contributed by the
clustering step itself, on a larger set of methods. We ask: (1) do HiSTaR's published DLPFC results reproduce with the
official code; (2) on a \emph{fixed} embedding, how much does ARI change with the initialisation of
the mixture model, and with perturbations that leave the data essentially unchanged (spot order,
negligible noise) under R mclust; (3) does selecting fits by likelihood help; and (4) how do \nMethodsPub{}
published methods and training-free baselines compare across three technologies, and does the ranking depend on how the
embeddings are clustered? The study design is summarised in
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}.

\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Data}
\textbf{DLPFC (10x Visium).} Twelve sections from three donors \citep{maynard2021dlpfc}; seven
annotated layers (five for donor 2), \texttt{layer\_guess\_reordered}.
\textbf{STARmap mouse medial prefrontal cortex.} Three sections (about 1050 cells, 166 genes) with
four annotated layers (L1, L2/3, L5, L6) \citep{wang2018starmap}.
\textbf{MERFISH mouse hypothalamus.} Animal 1, five sections from Bregma $-0.04$ to $-0.24$ (about
5500 cells, 155 genes) with eight annotated regions \citep{moffitt2018merfish}. The STARmap and
MERFISH annotations are those distributed with the BASS analyses \citep{li2022bass}. In total, \nSections{}
sections from three technologies.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
DLPFC: genes in fewer than 50 spots or with fewer than 10 counts were removed, 2000 highly
variable genes were selected (Seurat v3 flavour), counts were library-size normalised,
log-transformed and scaled, and reduced to 200 principal components (PCs). STARmap and MERFISH
(targeted panels): all genes, same normalisation, 50 PCs. All methods received the same input
where they accept one; BANKSY used the log-normalised genes, and SpaGCN, GraphST and the methods
added later (below) their own documented preprocessing of the same raw counts. The true number of domains was given to every method, and unannotated
spots were excluded from evaluation.

\subsection{Methods}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{HiSTaR} \citep{yu2025histar}: PyTorch port of the official code with the published
defaults (cross-level weight 0.3, 200+200 epochs).
\item \textbf{GraphST} \citep{long2023graphst}: official package and tutorial pipeline
(600 epochs, 20-PC embedding, R mclust EEE; with and without its label refinement). Because of its
CPU cost, GraphST was run with one seed on DLPFC and three seeds on STARmap and MERFISH.
\item \textbf{BANKSY} \citep{singhal2024banksy}: official \texttt{pybanksy}, 18 neighbours,
$m_{\max}=1$, $\lambda\in\{0.2,0.8\}$, 20 PCs.
\item \textbf{SpaGCN} \citep{hu2021spagcn}: official tutorial pipeline without histology
($p=0.5$, Louvain resolution search to the true number of domains, 200 epochs).
\item \textbf{Methods added in the extended study}, each run with its official code and tutorial
settings on one CPU thread: STAGATE \citep{dong2022stagate} (R mclust), SEDR \citep{xu2024sedr} (R mclust),
SpaceFlow \citep{ren2022spaceflow} (Leiden with a search for the true number of domains), NichePCA \citep{nichepca2025}
(Leiden, same search), CCST \citep{li2022ccst} and Spatial-MGCN \citep{wang2023spatialmgcn} (both $k$-means), DeepST
\citep{xu2022deepst} (Leiden with refinement), BASS \citep{li2022bass} and BayesSpace \citep{zhao2021bayesspace}
(Bayesian models that cluster internally; BayesSpace requires a spot lattice and was run on the
Visium sections only). NichePCA, SEDR, SpaceFlow and DeepST were run with three seeds per section; STAGATE, CCST, Spatial-MGCN and BASS
with one seed (seed 0) and BayesSpace once per section. Deviations from the tutorials (for example the neighbourhood radius,
expressed in spot spacings so that it transfers across technologies) are documented in the code
repository. Spatial-MGCN's released test script reports the epoch with the highest ARI against the
annotation; in the benchmark we report its final epoch (label-free) and give the label-selected value separately.
\item \textbf{Training-free baselines.} \emph{Linear diffusion}: the top 30 PCs are smoothed on the
spatial 6-nearest-neighbour graph, $H^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha)H^{(t)}+\alpha D^{-1}AH^{(t)}$,
$\alpha=0.5$, $T=10$. \emph{AnisoST}: the same with edge weights recomputed at every step,
$w_{ij}^{(t)}=\exp(-\|h_i^{(t)}-h_j^{(t)}\|^2/\kappa^{(t)})$ (Perona--Malik-type edge-preserving
diffusion \citep{perona1990}; $\kappa^{(t)}$ the median squared edge difference); a similar idea has
been used inside a deep model \citep{wang2024pearlst}. \emph{PCA} without smoothing is the
non-spatial reference. All baseline settings were fixed on DLPFC donor 1 before any other data
were analysed, and none were tuned on STARmap or MERFISH. All methods were run with default or published settings; none were tuned per dataset (equal tuning budgets are a topic for future work).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Clustering protocols}
\emph{Standard}: each method's usual clustering. This is R mclust for GraphST, STAGATE and SEDR, a 3-start
scikit-learn GMM \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn} with a fixed seed for HiSTaR, BANKSY and the baselines, Leiden
or $k$-means for the other methods, and the internal model for SpaGCN, BASS and BayesSpace.
\emph{Robust}: the method's embedding clustered with a tied-covariance Gaussian mixture (the mclust EEE model) with 20 random starts,
keeping the fit with the highest log-likelihood (label free); available for every method that outputs an
embedding. \emph{Lottery}: one seed-0 embedding per section and method, clustered 20 times with a
single random start each (\lotNMethods{} methods with embeddings). \emph{mclust perturbations}: R mclust (version 6.0.1, default
hierarchical initialisation) on the same embedding (i) unchanged, (ii) after random permutation of
the rows, 10 times, and (iii) after adding Gaussian noise with 1\% of each feature's standard
deviation, 10 times (\mcNMethods{} methods in the extended set, plus HiSTaR, GraphST and AnisoST).

\subsection{Evaluation and statistics}
ARI \citep{hubert1985ari} against the manual annotation; NMI gave the same conclusions (code
repository). For every (method, section) we average over seeds. Methods were compared with
two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over sections, with Holm correction \citep{holm1979} over the family of \nPooled{}
comparisons of linear diffusion with each method and clustering protocol. Run time and peak memory were measured
for a subset of methods in a standardised runner (process-tree sampling, one CPU thread per job).

\section{Results}

\subsection{HiSTaR's published DLPFC results did not reproduce}
With the official code and defaults, HiSTaR reached a median ARI over the twelve DLPFC sections of
0.509 (mean 0.491, 95\% bootstrap CI 0.454--0.525) with the 3-start GMM, and 0.449 (mean, seed 0)
when its embedding was clustered with R mclust as in the official pipeline, compared with a reported
median of 0.65 \todo{verify the reported values (0.65 median; 0.718 on 151673) against the HiSTaR
article}. Fixing a bug that applies random masking at inference did not change accuracy (0.487).
Possible causes include per-section tuning of the loss weights (described in the HiSTaR supplement),
unpublished preprocessing and the choice of seed; the following sections show that the clustering
step alone produces differences of this size. \todo{Report the outcome of contacting the authors.}

\subsection{On a fixed embedding, the clustering step alone moves ARI by up to \lotMax}
Figure~\ref{fig:lottery} shows the ARI of 20 single-start fits of the same mixture model on each
fixed embedding. Across the \lotN{} embeddings of \lotNMethods{} methods, the ARI range within an
embedding was \lotMean{} on average and reached \lotMax{} (\lotWorstMethod, \lotWorstSection). The effect depended
on the method: the mean range was \lotLo{} for \lotLoName{} and \lotHi{} for \lotHiName{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:lotmethods}); for HiSTaR and AnisoST it was
\lotMeanHistar{} and \lotMeanAniso{}, whereas GraphST's 20-dimensional PCA embeddings were more stable
(\lotMeanGraphst). The best of the 20 fits by ARI exceeded the
average single fit by \lotOracle: choosing a clustering run by its agreement with the labels, explicitly
or by trying a few seeds, would inflate a reported result by about as much as the gap between most
published methods.

\subsection{R mclust is deterministic but not stable}
mclust initialises EM by model-based hierarchical agglomeration, so one call on a fixed embedding
always returns the same partition. That partition, however, depended on details that carry no
information (Fig.~\ref{fig:mclust}). Over \mcN{} embeddings, randomly permuting the order of the spots changed ARI by
\mcPermMean{} on average within an embedding (up to \mcPermMax); on DLPFC the mean was \mcPermMeanDlpfc{} and on MERFISH \mcPermMeanMerfish. In \mcPermFrac\% of the permutations ARI moved by more than 0.05, and
for \mcPermGt\% of the embeddings the range exceeded 0.1. Adding noise at 1\% of each
feature's standard deviation had similar effects (mean range \mcJitMean, up to \mcJitMax; \mcJitMeanStarmap{} on STARmap). Over these embeddings, mclust's default fit had
a mean ARI of \mcDefault{} against \mcBest{} for the best-likelihood fit of 20 random starts, and its
log-likelihood was at least as high in only \mcLLfrac\% of cases.

\subsection{Choosing fits by likelihood is not a remedy}
A natural fix is to run many starts and keep the most likely fit. It helped some methods and
hurt others. Replacing each method's standard clustering by this matched protocol raised mean ARI for \nGainPos{} of
\nGainTot{} methods and lowered it for \nGainNeg. For example, it raised HiSTaR's mean ARI by \gainHistar{} (\gainWinsHistar/\gainNHistar{} sections,
\gainPHistar) and GraphST's by \gainGraphst{} (\gainWinsGraphst/\gainNGraphst, \gainPGraphst), but changed SEDR's by \gainSedr{}
and Spatial-MGCN's by \gainMgcn. The reason is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:lottery}: likelihood and ARI were positively
related on DLPFC (median Spearman $\rho$ \rhoDlpfcMed) but not on MERFISH (median $\rho$ \rhoMerfishMed; negative for
\rhoNegMerfish{} of \lotNMethods{} methods), where the best-fitting mixture can separate cell types rather than anatomical
regions. Better optimisation of the mixture model is therefore not the same as better domains.

\subsection{Under matched clustering, no deep encoder outperformed a training-free baseline}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Fig.~\ref{fig:matched} compare all methods under both protocols.
Across all methods and both protocols, the highest mean ARI was reached by \bestDlpfc{} on DLPFC (\bestDlpfcVal),
\bestStarmap{} on STARmap (\bestStarmapVal) and \bestMerfish{} on MERFISH (\bestMerfishVal). Linear graph diffusion had
a mean ARI of \meanLinDlpfc, \meanLinStarmap{} and \meanLinMerfish{} on the three datasets (average over datasets \overallLin, against \overallBass{} for BASS and \overallBanksyHi{} for BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$).
Over the \nSections{} sections it had a higher mean ARI than most competitors: the difference was significant after Holm
correction in \nPooledSig{} of the \nPooled{} comparisons, including HiSTaR with standard
clustering (+\dLinHistar, \winsLinHistar/20 sections, Holm-adjusted \pLinHistar), GraphST with its own mclust clustering (+\dLinGraphst, \winsLinGraphst/20, adjusted \pLinGraphst),
SpaGCN (+\dLinSpagcn, \winsLinSpagcn/20, \pLinSpagcn), NichePCA (+\dLinNichepca, adjusted \pLinNichepca), SpaceFlow (+\dLinSpaceflow, \pLinSpaceflow),
CCST (+\dLinCcst, \pLinCcst), DeepST (+\dLinDeepst, \pLinDeepst) and Spatial-MGCN (+\dLinMgcn, \pLinMgcn).
The differences to BANKSY $\lambda=0.8$ (+\dLinBanksyHi, adjusted \pLinBanksyHi), AnisoST (+\dLinAniso, \pLinAniso), SEDR (+\dLinSedr, \pLinSedr), BASS (+\dLinBass, \pLinBass), STAGATE
and HiSTaR with robust clustering (+\dLinStagateRob{} and +\dLinHistarRob, adjusted \pLinStagateRob{} and \pLinHistarRob) were not significant: these methods are
statistically indistinguishable from the baseline with \nSections{} sections, which is not evidence that they are equivalent. The ranking of the deep methods relative
to the baselines changed with the clustering protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:matched}). The deep methods
were also the least stable: HiSTaR's seed-to-seed standard deviation of ARI was 0.047 on DLPFC and
0.18 on STARmap, against 0.003--0.04 for the training-free baselines. Run time and memory differed by
orders of magnitude between methods (Table~\ref{tab:res}); in a separate feasibility test, one DLPFC section took
tens of minutes on one CPU thread for most of the neural methods and about 50 minutes for BayesSpace.

\subsection{One released script selects its reported epoch by agreement with the labels}
\todo{Contact the Spatial-MGCN authors and report their response before including this subsection.}
Spatial-MGCN's released test scripts (\texttt{DLPFC\_test.py}, \texttt{HBC\_test.py}) compute ARI against the annotation at every training epoch
and keep the best epoch for reporting. Applying the same selection rule in our runs (seed 0, \mgcnLeakN{} sections), this label-selected epoch had a mean ARI of
\mgcnOracleDlpfc{} on DLPFC, \mgcnOracleStarmap{} on STARmap and \mgcnOracleMerfish{} on MERFISH, against \mgcnFinalDlpfc, \mgcnFinalStarmap{} and
\mgcnFinalMerfish{} for the final epoch used in our benchmark, i.e.\ an inflation of \mgcnLeak{} on average (\mgcnLeakStarmap{} on STARmap).
Selecting a run, epoch or seed by agreement with the labels is a form of label leakage that makes a method look competitive with the training-free baselines
(\mgcnOracleDlpfc{} against \meanLinDlpfc{} on DLPFC) when its label-free result is not.

\subsection{Edge preservation helps at boundaries on Visium but not on single-cell data}
On DLPFC, AnisoST matched linear diffusion in ARI (0.551 vs 0.558) and improved accuracy on spots
at layer boundaries by 0.025 (9/12 sections, $p=0.034$, uncorrected) without changing interior
accuracy (Supplementary Fig.~S2). On MERFISH it was clearly worse (0.363 vs 0.497): at single-cell
resolution neighbouring cells often differ by cell type, so an edge-preserving rule blocks the
smoothing that is needed to reveal regions. We report this as a negative result for the
edge-preserving variant; the simpler linear diffusion was the more robust baseline.

\section{Discussion}
The final clustering step of spatial domain methods is a substantial and under-reported source of
variance. On fixed embeddings from \lotNMethods{} methods and three technologies, random initialisation alone spanned \lotMean{} ARI on average, and the R implementation used by most published methods changed its answer when the
spots were merely reordered. Better likelihood did not reliably mean better domains, and a training-free diffusion baseline matched or
outperformed every deep encoder we tested. These results agree with recent large benchmarks
\citep{explanatory2026,hu2024gbbench,nar2025bench} and add a direct, quantitative measurement of
clustering variance on fixed embeddings across a broader set of methods.

\paragraph{Recommendations.} (1) Report the clustering protocol: algorithm, implementation and
version, initialisation, number of starts, seeds, and how they were chosen. (2) Never select seeds,
epochs or runs by agreement with the labels. (3) Report distributions over training seeds \emph{and}
clustering runs (or input permutations), with per-section values. (4) Compare encoders under the
same clustering, and include training-free baselines such as neighbourhood smoothing. (5) Report
runtime, memory and seed variance alongside accuracy.

\paragraph{Limitations.} All methods were run with default or published settings (we did not tune
any method and we could not reproduce HiSTaR's per-section tuning), so our results
do not show what each method can reach with its authors' own tuning. Several methods were run with one seed, and
BayesSpace on Visium only. The lottery and mclust experiments use one embedding per section and method. The STARmap and MERFISH
annotations are coarse and the STARmap data contain only three small sections. Three technologies and \nSections{} sections
do not cover the breadth of spatial data (for example Slide-seq, Stereo-seq or Xenium). The number of domains
was given to every method. Annotations may themselves have been derived with methods similar to the ones evaluated.
